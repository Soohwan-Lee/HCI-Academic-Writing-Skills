# S08 — Critical position developed through evaluative questions

Source: [P111 When the Implication Is Not to Design](../analyses/P111-nondesign.md),
CHI 2011, four-page author copy. Entire main argument re-read 2026-10-31; prior
render inspection recorded in P111. This older short-paper contrast is useful for
argument form, not evidence of current venue requirements or an 8,000-word budget.

## 1. Actual hierarchy

The source uses argumentative headings rather than Introduction–Methods–Results:

1. Opening about HCI's expansion: locate a field-level tendency, state a position,
   preview three questions and explain the sustainability focus.
2. Articulating absence: low/no-tech alternatives; harm outweighing benefit;
   computational transformations of problems.
3. Discussion: opening positioning; solution space versus problem space; a further
   challenge to problem–solution framing itself.
4. Practice arguments: value non-design; explain unpursued paths; investigate
   removal; make negative results informative; retain critical building.
5. Acknowledgments and references.

The questions build an escalation: alternative means → possible harm → what the
technology represents as the problem. The discussion then interprets that escalation
and questions a premise shared by the preceding questions. The practical ending
makes the stance usable without claiming that the proposed practices were evaluated.
Literature is integrated where it supplies an example, concept, contrast or precedent.

## 2. Complete local paragraph map: low/no-tech subsection, pp.1–2

1. State the question, recall preexisting approaches, illustrate with a mundane
   alternative, qualify the claim about research value, and preview two cases.
2. Examine a sensor-gardening proposal: inherited local expertise, maintenance and
   disposal burdens, a social alternative and its possible collective consequences.
3. Examine producer/consumer connection: alternative media, then the deeper
   economic arrangement the intervention leaves untouched; hand off to the later
   computational-transformation question.

The two cases do not merely repeat “technology can be unnecessary.” The second
changes the scale of the critique and prepares the next kind of question. The
alternatives are argumentative illustrations, not comparative effect findings.

## 3. Complete local paragraph map: discussion, p.3

- Opening ¶1 establishes the field-level target, the examples' role and neighboring
  approaches. It prevents critique of a practice from becoming an attack on authors.
- Opening ¶2 compares nearby feature-omission, systemic-scale and ICT4D arguments,
  distinguishing what this paper adds.
- Solution/problem-space subsection, sole paragraph: define the two spaces and map
  the three questions onto their progression.
- Beyond-problems subsection ¶1: concede the usefulness of problem–solution
  evaluation, show where the analogy breaks and state the alternative understanding.
- Beyond-problems subsection ¶2: propose intervention-in-situation framing, explain
  its consequences, return to metrics/cases and identify possible wider relevance.

This is positioning → structured distinction → challenge to its shared premise →
reconstruction. The argument earns a stronger reframing gradually. Moving its final
claim to the first sentence of every subsection would remove that development.

## 4. Sentence-level maps

**Discussion opening, p.3, four sentences:** delimit target → name field-level object
→ explain example use → situate the approach. Each sentence clarifies the paper's
argumentative action. This boundary-setting is useful here because examples of named
systems have just been criticized; it is not a mandatory disclaimer for every essay.

**Solution/problem-space subsection, p.3, three sentences:** define familiar solution
exploration → introduce complementary problem-framing with parallel syntax → map
prior questions onto the progression and its design significance. Repeated “space”
and parallel operations maintain cohesion; the final sentence performs synthesis.

## 5. Conditional transfer

For critical/normative contributions, plan the premise, concrete situations,
reasoning, competing account and practical/intellectual consequence. A research
question can be an evaluative question rather than an empirical RQ with a participant
sample. Illustrative cases can earn a distinction without pretending to be systematic
coverage or outcome validation.

A conclusion may be a disciplined invitation to reconsider or refrain, not a new
artifact. Preserve the positive intellectual product: distinctions and questions
that change judgment. Other critical forms—genealogy, standpoint critique or
speculation—need their own source-based structural contrasts.
