# S06 — Literature synthesis that develops a relational model

Source: [P123 Digital Legacy](../analyses/P123-digital-legacy.md), CSCW 2023,
author PDF. Structure re-read 2026-10-31, with discussion pp.15–19 inspected in
text; those pages' visual review is recorded in P123. This is an interpretive
literature synthesis, not a pooled-effect meta-analysis. Source headings are
unnumbered; the hierarchy below must not be converted into invented section numbers.

## 1. Hierarchy and argumentative progression

| Section | Nested organization | New work done here |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction, pp.1–3 | Stakes → complexity → fragmented scholarship → synthesis preview | Earns the need to relate prior work |
| Methods, pp.3–7 | Scope/search/selection; qualitative analysis; two rounds explained within this account | Defines the corpus and how interpretations were developed |
| Findings, pp.7–15 | Definition analysis; identity; engagement; putting to rest; offline practices | Makes the literature's interests and differences intelligible |
| Discussion, pp.15–23 | Lifecycle → encoding/access/dispossession → dependencies → multiple users → generations → research/design challenges → social computing's role | Transforms themes into relationships, then derives questions and a disciplinary orientation |
| Limitations, p.23 | Scope and representational boundaries | Restricts coverage and interpretation |
| Conclusion, p.23 | Corpus and conceptual contribution | Recaps synthesis, not simply search effort |

The four thematic interests are not the three model stages. Findings and discussion
operate at different levels. There is no separate related-work section that repeats
the analyzed corpus: background positions the question, corpus papers provide
synthesis evidence, and lifecycle scholarship supplies a conceptual resource later.
A separate Related Work heading is optional when its job is already fulfilled.

## 2. Complete paragraph map: lifecycle introduction, pp.15–16

Before the stage-specific headings, six paragraphs build the model:

1. Notice a cross-theme relationship, connect it to lifecycle thinking, contrast
   examples, and name the three interpreted stages.
2. Explain information-lifecycle ancestry and its purpose.
3. Identify a closer social-computing lifecycle precedent.
4. Return to coded papers and show the resemblance using an example.
5. State departures from that precedent: encoding and access change the categories.
6. Consolidate the three-stage model, point to the figure, and explain its intended use.

The sequence alternates observation and conceptual resources. An AI should not
compress it into “three themes emerged, therefore we propose three stages.” The
source's intellectual work lies in the relation and transformation, not the number.
An alternative concise draft can combine ancestry paragraphs, but must preserve
credit and explain what changed.

## 3. Complete paragraph map: multi-user considerations, pp.18–19

1. Identify the limitation of reading a lifecycle as information-centered or
   single-user; state the multi-user correction.
2. Show that decisions anticipate others, with cases involving legacy contacts and
   recipients considering the deceased.
3. Add a different relation: the primary actor changes across stages; illustrate
   with an archive that can be deleted, passed on or re-encoded.
4. Test an apparent boundary case: an abandoned account can undergo dispossession
   without an intentional handoff.
5. A short closing paragraph turns the analysis into a future-support question.

The paragraphs are not five citations about multi-user systems. They successively
change what “multi-user” means: considering others, changing actors, and absent
intentional transmission. Figure 2 compares configurations rather than proving a
causal sequence. The next subsection changes the axis again, from people to generations.

## 4. Sentence-level maps

**Lifecycle opening, p.15, six sentences:** cross-theme observation → lifecycle
analogy during coding → dispossession example → encoding contrast → recurrence
across further reading → named synthesis. The middle examples earn the abstraction.
The next paragraphs establish its ancestry, so “emerged” should not imply an absence
of interpretive resources.

**Multi-user opening, p.18, three sentences:** possible information-centered
limitation → concrete single-user reading → multi-user correction. The paragraph
creates a question for the next three evidence paragraphs. It is effective because
it hands off a specific explanatory task; adding a generic importance sentence
would merely delay that task.

## 5. Conditional transfer

For a review with a model contribution, separate descriptive categories from the
relations the model proposes. Plan an explicit synthesis subsection where readers
can see how categories become stages, dimensions or dependencies. Follow it with
cases that exercise the model, including awkward cases, before the agenda.

A mapping review may instead organize around coverage and research configurations;
a critical review may foreground premises and political commitments; an effect
synthesis needs a different results architecture. Do not force all review papers
into this lifecycle form. See the review route for those distinctions and acquire
further structural maps before claiming typical review-section proportions.
