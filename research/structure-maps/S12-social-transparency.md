# S12 — Concept development through an illustrative design

Source: [P119 Social Transparency](../analyses/P119-social-transparency.md), CHI
2021, arXiv 2101.04719v1. Main text pp.1–24. Hierarchy follows the existing full-text
analysis; the complete §3 passage on pp.7–8 was re-read on 2026-10-31. Visual
inspection and other passage analysis are recorded in P119. This map covers selected
passages, not every paragraph in the paper.

## 1. Actual hierarchy and dependencies

| Section | Nested organization | Reader question and output |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction, §1 | Explainability problem and proposal | Why extend the object of explanation? |
| Related work, §2 | XAI; sociotechnical perspective; social-transparency lineage | What is inherited, and what changes when AI participates? |
| Scenario-based design exploration, §3 | Concept scope → method rationale → workshops → features → scenario choice → pilots | How was an initially broad concept made discussable? |
| Study methods, §4 | Recruitment; procedure; analysis | How were subsequent stakeholder interpretations elicited and analyzed? |
| Findings, §5 | Technical-explanation limits; three contextual levels; four design elements; transfer; challenges | What does the exploration reveal about the concept and its boundaries? |
| Discussion, §6 | Holistic explainability; human–AI assemblage; situated XAI | What changes in the theoretical account of explainability? |
| Limitations, §7; Conclusion, §8 | Scope and resulting position | What remains unresolved? |

The exemplary design enables conceptual inquiry. It is not a production system
whose effectiveness is established by participant reactions. §3 and §4 describe
different activities: concept/material development and the subsequent interview study.
Moving the visual-scenario design after the interviews would leave the elicitation
material unexplained; merging both activities into an undifferentiated user study
would obscure how the four design elements arose.

## 2. Complete local paragraph map: §3, pp.7–8

There are six prose paragraphs; the last begins with an inline design-choices label.
The third paragraph crosses the page boundary.

1. **Define and differentiate.** State the proposed addition to AI, explain deliberate
   breadth, acknowledge the inherited term, and distinguish prospective construction
   from an antecedent retrospective account. This turns the literature distinction
   into the problem the design exploration must solve.
2. **Set the activity's epistemic purpose.** Explain that the design supports formative
   concept development; introduce scenario-based design, explain its affordances,
   and connect them to the inquiry. The paragraph earns the method choice before
   reporting workshops.
3. **Trace emergence.** Introduce candidate domains, workshop participants and
   activities; give the kind of information participants requested; end with the
   convergent four-part design vocabulary. It moves from process to conceptual output.
4. **Interpret and instantiate.** Relate that vocabulary to group-awareness lineage,
   distinguish explanation of reasons, describe its representation, settle the
   feature combination and point to the visual scenario. The inherited framework
   is compared at the moment the new representation becomes concrete.
5. **Justify scenario and audience.** Explain the selected domain's accessibility
   and why both domain insiders and outsiders will participate. This prepares the
   recruitment logic of §4 rather than adding unrelated sample demographics.
6. **Specify consequential choices.** Describe pilots, information volume, the
   mapping from concepts to interface features, content origins and staged reveal.
   Close with the presentation procedure needed to interpret the interviews.

The progression is concept → epistemic purpose → emergence → representation →
audience → encounter. A topic-first opening works here because each paragraph
introduces the next dependency; it does not require repeating the paper's importance.

## 3. Sentence-level maps

**§2.3 final paragraph, p.7, four sentences:** inheritance and focal difference →
sharper distinction between visibility and AI explainability → conceptual warrant
for transferring a human–human lens → first-order scope and possible wider effects.
The referent chain keeps social transparency stable while changing its target.
The third sentence supplies the reason transfer is defensible; omitting it makes
novelty sound like merely applying an old label in a new domain.

**§3 paragraph 4, p.8, five sentences:** resemblance and decisive exception →
participant-proposed representation of that exception → selected feature bundle →
stable terminology for later use → figure anchor. The chain runs from the missing
“why” to textual reasons to the full design vocabulary. The closing figure reference
hands readers a concrete object; it is not an unsupported evaluative conclusion.

## 4. Conditional transfer and comparison

For concept-development work, a useful design-exploration subsection can explain
what must be made discussable, why this material/method serves that purpose, how
its elements emerged, what prior concept they resemble or depart from, and how
participants encountered them. Subsequent findings can develop conceptual dimensions
rather than rank interface quality.

Contrast S03: formative findings there motivate a delivered system and distinct
creator/recipient inquiries. Here the illustrative design is a vehicle for a
conceptual account. Contrast S05: a concept is extended through situated practice
rather than a deliberately constructed scenario. All three involve qualitative
material, but the material performs different argumentative jobs.
