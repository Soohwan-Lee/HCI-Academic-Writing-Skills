# S11 — Sociotechnical genealogy: eras, cross-era dimensions and methodological reflection

Source: [P115 digital sexual violence genealogy](../analyses/P115-dsv-genealogy.md),
CSCW 2026, source version identified there. Re-read §5.2 across PDF pp.19–21 on
2026-10-31; the main hierarchy and earlier visual inspection are documented in P115.
This map examines scholarly argument, not current legal guidance. Documentary
sources and author interpretations remain distinct.

## 1. Actual hierarchy

Introduction → related work (technologically facilitated violence; image-based
abuse; situated cultural history) → methodology (collection; analysis; positionality)
→ four eras → three cross-era dimensions → discussion (implications; reflection
on genealogy) → conclusion.

The eras section has nested treatment of two platform arrangements within one era.
The next major section deliberately reorganizes the material into dimensions:
constructed obscenity, imperceptibility, and commercial/industrial organization.
It is not another chronological summary. Discussion then considers consequences
for research/design and separately reflects on the analytical approach.

The architecture changes the organizing axis twice: time/context → analytical
relation → implications and method. It can establish continuities and ruptures
without treating each era as an inevitable technological advance. A chronological
heading is not evidence that one stage caused the next or that the archive is complete.

## 2. Complete paragraph map: imperceptibility dimension, §5.2, pp.19–21

1. Introduce a dominant consent framing, connect it to an earlier era, and question
   the awareness/agency assumptions of a voluntariness reading.
2. Supply a concrete documented example and reported institutional consequences.
3. Make a stronger normative/conceptual argument against that framing and introduce
   imperceptibility as the proposed analytical focus.
4. State the cross-era interpretation and explain the change of focus.
5. Develop one dimension: whether the initiation of harm can be perceived; compare
   examples across eras.
6. Add another dimension: how distributed actors and infrastructures obscure the
   progression of harm.
7. Add temporality: delayed or absent awareness changes an event into a continuing
   condition in the authors' account.
8. Add collective practices: community participation changes how harm is organized
   and experienced as visible or invisible.
9. Return to institutional framing, use a legal example as interpreted in the paper,
   and articulate why the preceding dimensions challenge its assumed moments/actors.

The first four paragraphs prepare the conceptual shift; the next four unpack it;
the last returns it to a consequential domain. This is more specific than “theme,
quotes, implications.” Paragraph 3 is normative reasoning, while paragraphs 5–8
combine documentary contrasts and interpretation. Their different warrants should
remain visible in a draft.

The source's categorical consent argument and progressive language have tensions
recorded in P115. The structure map does not endorse every proposition, infer victim
experience directly from secondary reports, or adopt the legal interpretation as a
current rule. Strong rhetoric still needs its own warrant.

## 3. Sentence-level maps

**§5.2 first paragraph, p.19, six sentences:** locate the dominant concept → link
to an era-specific reading → expose an assumed moment of possible awareness/refusal
→ explain omitted constraints → identify a consequence of the assumption → synthesize
its relation to power and agency. Repeated awareness/choice concepts connect the
reasoning. The paragraph moves beyond description into critique; attribution and
analytical agency matter.

**§5.2 third paragraph, p.19, four sentences:** escalate from a narrower problem
to a conceptual thesis → state the thesis's premise → give a normative counterclaim
→ redirect the inquiry. This is not a new empirical finding. In revision, check
whether the strong thesis follows from the stated premise and whether the remainder
of the manuscript uses the challenged concept consistently.

## 4. Conditional transfer

For genealogy or historical interpretation, consider first making temporal contexts
legible, then crossing them to develop dimensions. An alternative can integrate
interpretation within each period, but needs an explicit cross-period synthesis
somewhere if relationships across periods are the contribution.

Give documentary collection, interpretive procedure and positionality appropriate
space; do not rename the contribution a systematic review solely because it reads
published material. Conversely, conceptual/normative claims require explicit reasoning
rather than inheriting empirical authority from a large source count. A separate
method-reflection subsection is useful when the inquiry also teaches how a field
can use the approach, without claiming it outperforms alternatives.
