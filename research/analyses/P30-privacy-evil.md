# P30 — “So-called privacy breeds evil”

**Source and scope.** Rosanna Bellini, Emily Tseng, Nora McDonald, Rachel Greenstadt, Damon McCoy, Thomas Ristenpart, and Nicola Dell, *“So-called privacy breeds evil”: Narrative Justifications for Intimate Partner Surveillance in Online Forums*, CSCW3 2020, article 210, 27 pages, DOI 10.1145/3432909. Read the proceedings-formatted PDF mirrored by BISC-MI, with body through p. 22. Official [CSCW 2020 awards](https://cscw.acm.org/2020/awards/) lists Best Paper. Selected for privacy/abuse, naturalistic narratives, and a model built from interpretive analysis. One analyst codes this version; no style–award inference is made.

**Knowledge object and methods.** Author-stated contributions comprise storytelling practices, five story functions, 21 justifications, and a four-stage account of changing attitudes toward intimate partner surveillance (IPS). The primary route is qualitative empirical explanation; a grounded conceptual framework is an independently articulated secondary knowledge object. Narrative analysis and grounded theory are complementary methods, not independent artifacts. The data are 556 story posts extracted from a previously collected forum dataset. The authors clearly distinguish this paper's question about motivations and justifications from prior work cataloging attack methods. Counts organize the analyzed material; they do not establish population rates of perpetration.

**Architecture and opening.** Introduction → Related Work → Constructing a Data Set of IPS Narratives → Narrative Analysis of IPS Stories (methods followed by four findings questions) → Grounded Theory on Users of Intimate Partner Surveillance (methods, findings/model) → Discussion → Conclusion. This is a shared-data, two-analysis architecture, not one universal Method–Results block. The abstract presents the harm/context, survivor-perspective gap, qualitative focus, corpus, findings, model, and intervention implications. The introduction (pp. 1–2) narrows from IPV to IPS and online disclosure, states exactly what the team's earlier study left unanswered, previews detailed findings and the model, then lists contributions and implications. There are no numbered RQs even though finding headings are questions.

**Related work.** Four strands justify distinct decisions: IPS literature establishes the technical and survivor-focused landscape; narrative/abuse scholarship motivates the analytic object and comparison to existing violence models; stigmatized online disclosure explains why forums can expose otherwise inaccessible accounts; infidelity-forum work supports the sampling site and differentiates this analysis from earlier data collection. The argument acknowledges valuable survivor research rather than treating it as a defective approach. Its contribution is an additional perspective on self-reported conduct, not access to unmediated perpetrator truth.

**Claim–support–warrant–limit.** Narrative structures, categorized reasons for posting, paraphrased stories, and coding-derived categories support the explanation of how surveillance is justified and normalized. The model links expectations, changed thinking, escalation, and reflection with reported influences and repetitions. Its warrant is interpretive consistency across accounts, not longitudinal observation or experimental causation. The discussion explicitly calls for independent validation before predictive use. The paper avoids definitive perpetrator/victim labels for unverifiable posters and uses poster/target terminology. Pronoun-based gender inference, deleted moderation content, unknown audiences, and self-presentation constrain conclusions. Suggested interventions require safety-sensitive future work; the study has not demonstrated their efficacy. Ethical reporting choices—pseudonyms and rewording—are part of the evidentiary presentation, and the source stories should not be reproduced as traceable quotations in a writing exercise.

**Micro-example 1: bounding the object.** Visually checked p. 5, §2, opening “We want to be”: four sentences. S1 states the boundary against condemning all reassurance-seeking; S2 supplies benign forum uses; S3 contrasts these with abuse-related material; S4 specifies prior filtering and this paper's subset. Relations: scope qualification → counterexample → contrast → operational boundary. The preceding paragraph states the new analysis of an existing corpus; the next section explains narrative extraction. This paragraph protects the study's unit of analysis before operationalizing it, rather than opening with an empirical conclusion.

**Micro-example 2: theory comparison.** Visually checked p. 17, §5.2, opening “In generating this theory”: four sentences. S1 introduces limited similarities to prior models; S2 identifies a concrete overlap with tension-building and rationalization; S3 contrasts the model with calm/reconciliation stages; S4 proposes continuous digital coercive control as the better interpretation. Relations: comparison → evidence of overlap → distinction → synthesized claim. The prior paragraph warns that stages can repeat and need not all occur; the following paragraph begins the stage-by-stage account. The concluding interpretation is earned through comparison, not merely restated from S1.

**Transfer and exception.** For reanalysis, name the previous question and the genuinely new question before asserting novelty. For complementary analyses, explain the different inferential job of each and maintain local method–finding proximity. A stage diagram should not silently convert retrospective narratives into a validated behavioral predictor. This architecture is unnecessary when one analysis answers one bounded question; it supplies no universal paragraph-size or 8,000-word allocation rule.
