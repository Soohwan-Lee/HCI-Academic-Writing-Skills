# P103 — Experimental group-AI claims with experiential tradeoffs

**Source and selection.** Chiang, Lu, Li, and Yin, *Enhancing AI-Assisted Group Decision Making through LLM-Powered Devil’s Advocate*, IUI 2024,pp 103–119, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3640543.3645199). Read the full substantive text of the 17-page [NSF-hosted published PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10524816). Selected for a group-level experimental unit, explicit intervention contrasts, and divergent performance/experience outcomes. Purdue affiliation is provenance, not proof of writing quality. No award claim. One analyst coded the argument and visually checked two entire paragraphs; separate supplemental materials were not included in this reading.

## Knowledge claim and architecture

The main contribution is experimental knowledge about designs for dissent in AI-assisted group decisions. Four LLM configurations operationalize objection target (AI versus initial human majority) and interactivity (static versus dynamic), alongside a no-advocate control. Implementing these conditions enables the comparison; it is not automatically an independent systems contribution. Exploratory chat analysis supplies possible mechanisms and interaction problems rather than a second causal experiment.

Actual sections are Introduction; Related Work (group discussion, AI-assisted decisions, LLMs); Study Design (RQs, task, treatments, procedure, measurements); Results (four RQ subsections and exploratory analysis); Discussion (dynamic-AI findings, majority-target limitations, LLM challenges, study limitations); Conclusion; acknowledgments; references. Four RQs appear at the start of study design, not in the introduction. The two recruitment phases are not two studies: Phase 1 establishes a participant pool; Phase 2 contains example tasks and the group experiment.

The abstract progresses through relevance, inappropriate AI use, the question, intervention dimensions, qualified comparative findings, workload/experience differences, and implications. Introduction pp 103–104 moves from group intelligence and AI promise to overreliance, devil's advocacy and its human costs, a proposed LLM role, design factors, the experiment, conditional results, and significance. Related work establishes dissent's rationale and authenticity problem, distinguishes appropriate reliance from agreement, and shifts the unit from individual-AI interaction to group-AI interaction. The LLM subsection positions a conversational mechanism rather than treating model novelty as sufficient justification.

## Evidence, warrants, limits

The analyzed 350 completers formed 120 groups, initially three people each; some continued with two after dropout or inactivity removal. Eight formal tasks per group concern recidivism prediction using a deliberately biased assistant. The authors standardize outcomes by task instance and aggregate group decision measures; individual ratings measure perceived workload and interaction quality. Those units must remain visible in writing. Group accuracy is operationalized as appropriate reliance, while correct-AI and wrong-AI cases further separate underreliance from overreliance.

The strongest reported accuracy comparison concerns dynamic objection to AI versus control. Several other contrasts are marginal or nonsignificant, including the proposed reduction of overreliance on out-of-distribution cases. Do not compress this into a reliable general reduction of overreliance. Perceived teamwork quality and self-rated performance can be lower in the condition with higher decision accuracy. Longer discussion and qualitative coding offer a plausible deliberation account, not established causal mediation. The paper's initial language suggesting genuine LLM viewpoints or preserved psychological safety should not be adopted as an empirically demonstrated mechanism.

Limits include one domain, a selected AI failure pattern, anonymous short-lived nonexpert groups, and unmeasured reasons for dropout. These are transfer boundaries, especially for continuing teams with status hierarchies. The ethics/debrief account concerns the experimental manipulation; this paper does not validate real-world recidivism deployment.

## Two paragraph micro-analyses

**Printed p 115/PDF 13, §5.1, “Another somewhat puzzling observation” (six sentences).** S 1 juxtaposes decision accuracy and perceived performance; S 2 adds teamwork ratings; S 3 labels a discord explanation as conjecture; S 4 extends that conjecture using discussion intensity; S 5 abstracts a design-goal tension; S 6 proposes further work. The preceding paragraph bounds the distribution-specific result; the following subsection considers majority-target null results. This is observation-first and contrast-driven. The paragraph preserves a tradeoff instead of forcing every metric into a success narrative, while its conjectural sentences should remain marked as such.

**Printed p 116/PDF 14, §5.4, “While our study sheds” (five sentences).** S 1 bounds online findings against other interaction modes; S 2 explains mode diversity; S 3 lists anonymity, short duration, and expertise conditions; S 4 identifies concrete contrasting situations; S 5 states the resulting transfer concern. The preceding paragraph addresses hallucination and responsibility; the following paragraph adds domain and dropout limits. Its opening is a scope claim, not merely a topic label. Specific design conditions make the limitation useful to a reader planning an extension.

**Transfer.** Align RQs, contrasts, measurement units, and result headings; report uncertainty and experiential costs alongside accuracy. A group intervention's explanatory narrative must distinguish manipulated factors, measured moderators, and speculative mechanisms.
