# P35 — “They Just Don’t Get It”

**Source and selection.** To, Sweeney, Hammer, and Kaufman, PACMHCI 4 CSCW1 article 24 (2020), *Towards Social Technologies for Coping with Interpersonal Racism*, DOI 10.1145/3392828. Author-hosted proceedings PDF; Best Paper verified on the official CSCW 2020 awards page. Full substantive text and open-code appendix read; p. 24:20 visually checked. Single-analyst analysis. This replaces an excluded candidate, *Daughters of Men*: the available repository PDF was an anonymous draft with placeholder citation and materially different themes, so it was not attributed to the final award-winning publication.

**Contribution and support.** Primary contribution is qualitative empirical understanding of support-seeking after interpersonal racism, especially uncertainty, trusted supporters, and audience curation. Fourteen narrative-episode and semi-structured interviews supply the main evidence; the ethnic-identity measure characterizes participants, while a technology-use survey is not separately reported because it repeats interview findings. These instruments do not create independent quantitative contributions. The grounded-theory coding process supports interpretive relationships; the CARE project name does not denote an evaluated system. Future co-design, cultural probes, and effectiveness evaluation are explicitly future work.

**Actual sequence.** Introduction → Background (race/racism theories; social support-seeking) → Methods (recruitment, participants, procedure, reflexive statement) → Analysis → Findings (experiences, representative stories, communication/support processes) → Discussion (sharing experience, identifying supporters, trust/agency) → Limitations → Future Work → Conclusion → acknowledgements/references → code appendix. Analysis has its own section because it explains how narrative material becomes concepts. Findings first preserve event context through representative accounts, then organize the cross-case interpretation. Treating all of that as a compact generic Results section would obscure the evidentiary path.

**Opening moves.** The abstract introduces harm and population context, states the current inquiry and long-term ambition separately, names narrative interviews, previews uncertainty and distrust of public platforms, and gives design implications. Introduction pp. 24:1–3 starts with a participant epigraph, establishes the social problem and HCI's limited attention, acknowledges a strong adjacent support literature, then asks exploratory questions. It bounds the present step in the CARE program, states two RQs, explains the interview approach, limits the focus relative to overt violence and systemic injustice, and previews findings. Importantly, desired future support and the present empirical contribution remain separate.

**Related-work function.** Race theory is essential context for interpreting ambiguous everyday experiences and intersectionality, not decorative background. Social-support research supplies known benefits and disclosure mechanisms, then introduces risks of harassment, invalidation, and audiences. The review ends by connecting those tensions to choices of whether, where, how, and with whom to seek support. It acknowledges prior online-racism work while distinguishing studying its operation from supporting those coping with it. This is a bounded gap claim, not erasure of adjacent marginalized-community scholarship.

**Claim–warrant–limit.** Participant accounts of uncertainty, selective sharing, trusted relationships, and repeated network curation support the interpretation of coping as an ongoing social process. Detailed stories let readers inspect how practical risks and meaning-making interrelate; coding explains the move to themes. The paper explicitly distinguishes the investigators' uncertainty interpretation from participants' own terminology. Retrospective accounts cannot establish a longitudinal sequence for everyone, rank population-wide motivators, or show that proposed tools improve coping. The sample is college-educated, primarily from one U.S. city, self-selected for discussing racism, and high in racial-ethnic identity affiliation. These limits matter to design transfer, particularly to less practiced or less comfortable support-seekers.

**Micro-analysis.** Discussion §6.3, p. 24:20, opening locator “First, the question”: S1 identifies difficulty assessing trustworthy supporters online; S2 states the possible usefulness of cues; S3 gives a contextual assurance example; S4 introduces the risk that cues misrepresent understanding; S5 elaborates malicious or inadequate support; S6 supplies a more extreme co-option example; S7 derives a protective navigation requirement while retaining the benefit of broad access. Relations: topic/tension → affordance → example → qualification → risk elaboration → escalated example → implication. This is not uniformly deductive: the strongest design conclusion comes last. The preceding two-sentence paragraph establishes trust as a barrier; the next paragraph shifts from trust in people to trust in platforms. Adjacent paragraphs partition trust's object, producing progression rather than repeating the keyword.

**Writing lesson and exception.** Use future-work boundaries to prevent a design-motivated empirical study from sounding like an intervention evaluation. A strong introduction can include an epigraph, preliminary questions, formal RQs, scope limitation, and findings preview without obeying a single fixed funnel. Preserve conceptual uncertainty and researcher positionality where they affect interpretation; neither award status nor detailed coding removes evidence limits or establishes prose-length norms.
