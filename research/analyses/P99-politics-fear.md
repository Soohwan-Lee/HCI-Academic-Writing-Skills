## Claim, method, and architecture

The contribution is an interpretive account of fear and religious minority participation, with conceptual consequences for restorative justice and interfaith communication. Twenty interviews conducted over six months are the evidence-generating method, not six months of longitudinal observation of each participant. The findings concern narrated experiences in Bangladesh, not estimated population prevalence. The abstract moves from an underdeveloped religious-minority perspective to its relevance, the interview study, theoretical lenses, interpreted relationships, and discussion contributions. Its opening is a literature-gap claim rather than a broad technological history.

The actual sequence is Introduction; Literature Review; Research Site; Methods (collection, analysis, positionality); Findings (silencing, institutional politics of fear, coping); Discussion (restorative justice, interfaith coexistence, harm narratives, double consciousness, limitations); Conclusion; acknowledgments; references. The separate historical site section is necessary argument infrastructure. Introduction pp 2–4 progresses through public-sphere promise and concrete violence, why faith-specific experience cannot simply be substituted with other minority categories, the study, fear as an organizing concept, and two contribution bullets. The list crosses a page boundary; it does not supply a universal introduction template or require numbered RQs.

Related work pp 4–7 connects three bodies: social/political psychology of fear, marginalization in public deliberation, and restorative justice. Within them, definitions establish an interpretive resource; contrasts expose the assumptions behind otherwise constructive participation interventions. The important gap concerns whether safe expression and equal standing can be presumed, rather than whether another country has been studied. The review also reserves conceptual vocabulary used later, reducing the need to introduce every theoretical term inside quotations.

## Evidence and interpretive warrants

Methods pp 8–11 explain recruitment, trust-sensitive interviewing, translation, collaborative inductive coding, subsequent theory selection, and researcher positionality. The chronological distinction between inductive analysis and later theoretical interpretation matters: findings arranged under theories are not evidence that the interviews tested those theories prospectively. The authors explicitly do not report intercoder reliability. The findings combine a theoretical lens, participant accounts, interpretation, and transitions to another social scale. Supportive majority-community actions qualify the dominant account of marginalization.

The warrant for discussion is that reported silence and anticipated repercussions complicate remedies requiring victims to speak or seek justice. Proposed aftercare and trusted mediation remain proposals; their effectiveness was not evaluated. Limited age diversity, relatively homogeneous demographics, within-faith differences, and interview-based access bound transfer. Table 1 includes a Christian participant although the abstract names Hindu, Buddhist, and Indigenous communities: preserve the full sampling account when summarizing.

## Two paragraph-level observations

**p 12, §5.1, “People feel increasing pressure” (six sentences).** S 1 states the general fear/suppression relationship; S 2 attributes the named theory; S 3 explains environmental monitoring; S 4 contrasts silence with articulation; S 5 clarifies the anticipated sanction; S 6 extends the mechanism over time through media. This is deductive conceptual exposition, not an empirical conclusion from this sample. The preceding paragraph maps the three findings lenses; the following subsection narrows the mechanism to perceived opinion distribution before presenting observations and a quotation. A findings section can therefore contain a short conceptual orientation when its evidentiary status is clear.

**p 22, §6.1.2, “Trust plays a crucial” (nine sentences).** S 1 establishes a cited premise; S 2 specifies its social process; S 3 introduces a barrier; S 4 explains its consequence; S 5 connects the study's negative cases; S 6 supplies a positive counterpoint; S 7 synthesizes findings with prior scholarship; S 8 proposes a design direction by analogy; S 9 bridges to interfaith communication. The preceding subsection develops aftercare; the following paragraph changes the discussion scale to polarization. This is premise-first reasoning rather than a paper-result-first opening. The move from accounts to an AR-related proposal is an inferential extension, not a demonstrated intervention effect.

**Reusable lesson.** Make the contextual assumptions of a design ideal explicit, preserve supportive exceptions, and distinguish interpretation from remedy validation. Do not copy the paper's many theories as a quota or treat geographic specificity as self-sufficient novelty.
