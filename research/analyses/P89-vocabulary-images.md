The contribution is empirical knowledge about augmenting a learning technique, enabled by a study application—not a novel image-generation algorithm.

## Structure and rhetorical progression

The sequence is Abstract → Introduction → Related Work → The Overall Research Method → Study A: Externalizing Mental Visualization and Image Suitability → Study B: Comparing Image Generators → Study C: Comparing Keyword Methods → Discussion → Conclusion → Acknowledgments → References → Appendices A–C. Each study supplies its own participants, analysis, and results. Appendices document vocabulary selection, detailed image ratings, and example associations.

The abstract (pp. 1–2) explains the keyword mnemonic, identifies the difficulty of retaining implicit visual links, introduces externalization, then follows the dependency chain across three studies before claiming retention benefits. Introduction (pp. 2–3) develops memory and vocabulary stakes, makes the mnemonic understandable through concrete examples, hypothesizes benefits from visible images, and identifies previously unavailable imagery as the enabling gap. Generator availability then motivates the study sequence. RQs appear in the overall-method section (p. 6), where four questions map to three studies: feasibility and suitability, generator selection, and comparative learning outcomes.

Related work separates mnemonic learning from image synthesis. The first supplies the learning mechanism and contrary findings, including low-imageability vocabulary; the second explains the candidate technologies and why a selection study is needed. This supports a technology-enabled empirical question rather than treating the latest generator as the contribution. Some background is historical inventory; the transferable feature is the bridge from variation across generators to the need for Study B, not a rule that every model needs an equal-length description.

## Evidence and limits

Study A uses ten participants to test description and image suitability; Study B uses fourteen to select among generated images; Study C compares methods within 32 participants and tests recall immediately and seven days later. Word sets and condition order are counterbalanced. Language groups also differ in recruitment location and background, so their differences cannot establish a clean language or cultural mechanism.

The clearest supported effect is delayed unaided recall: 42.50% with images versus 29.06% without. Immediate recall is numerically higher but not statistically significant in the reported table. Image generation also takes more learning time; learning-efficiency measures do not show a significant method effect. Thus “improves learning” needs outcome, interval, and effort qualifications. Participants choose among images; all generation is manually triggered by researchers behind an apparently automated interface. This validates a learning arrangement, not a fully deployed automated product. Concrete high-imageability words, selected populations, two testing occasions, and historically specific generators bound transfer. The draft contains inconsistent statistical prose/table values and participant-count wording; its tables and final publication require checking before quotation. The introductory claim of faster fluency is motivation, not a measured outcome.

## Transferable moves

Use a study sequence to resolve prerequisites before the outcome comparison. Explain why one study changes the next study’s implementation. Separate preference, suitability, recall, elapsed effort, and efficiency. Carry null results and costs into the synthesis. A multi-study empirical paper can include an application and technical background without turning into a systems contribution; its organization should follow the questions and dependencies that establish the learning claim.
