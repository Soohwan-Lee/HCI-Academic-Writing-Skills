# P77 — A Fieldwork of the Future with User Enactments

## Source and contribution

Odom, Zimmerman, Davidoff, Forlizzi, Dey, and Lee, DIS 2012, DOI 10.1145/2317956.2318008. The ten-page author-hosted accepted-revision PDF identifies Carnegie Mellon's Human-Computer Interaction Institute; its filename and lack of proceedings pagination distinguish it from the publisher layout. Odom's publication list explicitly reports a Best Paper Award; this is author-reported primary evidence, not an independently inspected conference award list. All main text was read, and two complete paragraphs were inspected as rendered pages. Coding is single-analyst and version-dependent.

The primary contribution is methodological refinement: when and how to use user enactments, plus an account of enactments as boundary objects. The method of producing that knowledge is retrospective reflection across three applications over five years. The paper acknowledges that user enactments already existed; its contribution is not inventing them anew. The studies concern smart-home parenting with eighteen parents, family reminders with twelve families, and virtual possessions with fourteen teenagers. Their concepts are vehicles for eliciting values and improving a method, not three independently validated products.

## Architecture and opening moves

The sequence is Introduction; Background, containing design process, evolution, and Related Work; Lessons Learned from the UE Process, first describing three studies and selected enactments, then five methodological themes; Discussion and Reflection; Conclusion; acknowledgments and references. The themes are control, fidelity, ideation/talkback, intentional provocation, and attention/subtlety. Presenting cases before cross-case lessons gives later prescriptions identifiable grounding.

The abstract establishes why current knowledge poorly predicts desirable technological futures, introduces enactments as an approach, specifies reflection on repeated use, and previews methodological lessons and boundary-object interpretation. The introduction expands the same problem into a design question, explains simulated social context, qualifies benefits with iteration costs, states two contributions, and supplies a roadmap. Its gap concerns uncertainty in approaching unfamiliar design spaces; it is not an empirical hypothesis that enactments produce a measurable improvement.

Related Work identifies inheritance from scenario-based design, experience prototyping, performance, and value-oriented approaches. It then distinguishes multiple futures and designer-prepared scenarios. The distinction is conditional: the paper suggests some participatory approaches may be less suitable for particular emerging spaces, rather than establishing universal superiority. The Background also critiques the authors' own earlier matrix-based process. This gives novelty a concrete before/after methodological basis and prevents citations from functioning merely as a list of neighboring techniques.

## Claim, support, warrant, and limits

The central claim is that enacted futures can reveal values and design opportunities when appropriately staged and iterated. Support includes scenario descriptions, unexpected participant reactions, failed attempts, and subsequent changes to staging. The warrant links those visible consequences to methodological requirements. For instance, under-directing and over-directing teenagers yield different performance problems, supporting a calibrated-control lesson. Different fidelity needs across family and bedroom cases support contextual judgment rather than a universal realism threshold.

These are practice-based arguments, not comparative estimates of risk reduction or predictive validity. Ambient concepts were eventually dropped because directing attention undermined their intended peripheral character. Provocation sometimes taught the team about participants' lives without supporting deployment of the provocative concept. The boundary-object claim is illustrated through team coordination and subsequent development; it is not established through an independent team comparison. These distinctions matter when drafting a method paper with rich cases but no controlled evaluation.

S2 reports appreciation contrary to anticipated offense. S3 supplies participants' accounts of unnoticed successful care and memorable mistakes. S4 limits the inference: the outcome was richer understanding of parenting, not a recommendation that technology validate people. The preceding paragraph describes a contrasting teen response; the following paragraph synthesizes both as insight-producing provocations. Its final sentence is the epistemic payoff and boundary, not a decorative summary.

## Transferable choices

Make a method's evolution and abandoned assumptions explicit. Revisit named cases when deriving lessons so readers can follow the inference. Report failures that delimit applicability, and distinguish elicitation success from product desirability. This case-to-principle architecture is appropriate for reflective methodological contributions; it does not excuse thin reporting of participants, procedure, or analytic choices in a standalone empirical study.
