# P39 — Lessons from the Woodshop

**Source and scope.** Dew and Rosner, CHI 2018 paper 585, *Lessons from the Woodshop: Cultivating Design with Living Materials*. University of Washington hosted proceedings PDF, DOI 10.1145/3173574.3174159. The proceedings header explicitly marks Honorable Mention. Full substantive text read; pp. 4 and 9 rendered and checked. Single-analyst rhetorical interpretation; this copy supports neither venue-wide proportions nor universal sentence templates.

**Contribution versus method.** Eighteen months of apprenticeship-led ethnographic inquiry in timber framing supports design knowledge about living materials. The authors explicitly offer five lessons and three methodological orientations. Although the fieldwork includes building three tiny houses, these are not novel interactive systems being evaluated, and participation in making alone does not make the contribution an artifact. Primary classification here is empirically grounded design knowledge; the interpretive case is its warrant. The methodological orientations concern how designers attend to materials, not a validated new data-collection instrument. The study includes three intensive workshops, conversations with over 20 builders, approximately ten hours of interviews with eight informants, and visual/embodied records.

**Actual structure.** Abstract → Introduction → Background (Living Materials; Expanding Material Properties) → Method → Characteristics of Living Materials (knots, wood grain, sap, tusk tenon, scarf joint) → Discussion (material recuperation, more-than-human actors/timescales, properties as prototyping sites) → Conclusion. The findings heading names the knowledge offered rather than using a generic Results heading. The discussion explicitly returns to three introduction questions and moves from observed craft to proposed HCI practices.

**Opening moves.** The abstract begins directly with the ethnography, then identifies its revealing case properties, HCI relevance, five lessons, and three future areas. It has no obligatory opening problem sentence. Introduction pp. 1–2 starts with growing HCI interest in changing materials, identifies limitations of passive production models, and asserts that materials have histories before designers arrive. It then introduces timber framing as an informative alternative practice, gives fieldwork scope, locates prior work on decline and time, asks three questions, and states two contributions. Relevance is constructed through a conceptual tension and site justification, not a claim that nobody has studied wood.

**Related-work argument.** Background first establishes an active-materials conversation and then traces changing accounts of properties, from relational affordances to enacted qualities. Prior craft studies show what situated inquiry has already achieved. The final synthesis specifies how timber framing adds detail about finite resources, material histories, and engagements beyond assembly. This is theory-building positioning through an empirical case; citing each school is useful because the distinctions govern the later reading of grain, decay, and repair. The authors acknowledge that some lessons may seem obvious to woodworkers, locating novelty relative to HCI rather than claiming discovery of craft itself.

**Claim–support–warrant–limit.** Specific episodes—rejecting a load-bearing knot, constructing a joint that tolerates movement, making repairs visible—support claims about temporal and relational material properties. Photographs, practitioner explanations, and first-person learning help readers follow the interpretation. Extended-case reasoning connects these observations to existing material theories. Proposed directions for digital fabrication are analogical and generative, not measured improvements in sustainability or usability. The geographically specific craft program, first author's participation/sponsorship, and situated vocabulary condition transfer. No separate limitations section appears; that absence does not remove the analyst's duty to distinguish observed practices from proposed technical applications.

**Micro-analysis A.** Findings p. 4, opening locator “Although our claim”: S1 anticipates that naming characteristics could imply fixed properties, then defines the intended temporary meaning; S2 qualifies the discrete presentation by stressing interdependence; S3 explains how wood, environment, and history jointly produce the qualities; S4 synthesizes the expanded analytic scope. Relations: anticipated objection → qualification → explanatory warrant → synthesis. This is a qualification-first paragraph, not a summary claim followed by examples. The preceding paragraph explains how themes were distilled; the following subsection starts the first characteristic. The paragraph prevents the list format from contradicting the paper's theory.

**Micro-analysis B.** Discussion p. 9, opening locator “Taking a lesson”: S1 reframes properties as temporary situated relationships; S2 derives their openness to reworking; S3 defines the special use of prototyping; S4 specifies opportunities created by this interpretation. Relations: conclusion → inference → terminology clarification → implication. Here the opening is genuinely conclusion-first. The preceding paragraph contrasts predictable fabrication models with woodshop observations; the following paragraph proposes speculative tools and asks design questions. The small definition paragraph mediates the move from empirical contrast to design agenda.

**Transferable exception.** A strong empirical-design argument can make familiar manual practice informative for unfamiliar technical settings. Explicitly mark the inferential crossing: observed episode, analytic characteristic, then possible design orientation. Treat definitions and qualifications as substantive paragraph jobs; forcing every paragraph into claim–example–takeaway would erase necessary conceptual care.
