# P104 — Field intervention evidence and the meaning of reflection

**Source.** Terzimehić, Haliburton, Greiner, Schmidt, Hussmann, and Mäkelä, *MindPhone: Mindful Reflection at Unlock Can Reduce Absentminded Smartphone Use*, DIS 2022,pp 1818–1830, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3532106.3533575). Read the complete substantive text of the 13-page [author-hosted conference PDF](https://www.lukehaliburton.com/files/publications/Terzimehic%20et%20al_2022_MindPhone.pdf). Selected for an explicit reflective-intervention comparison, field deployment and post-removal observations, and tension between quantitative similarity and experiential differences. No award claimed. Single-analyst rhetorical coding; two complete paragraphs visually checked.

## Claim and section sequence

The paper offers intervention design and empirical knowledge about prompting attention beyond the phone. It names system, empirical, and conceptual contributions. These should not automatically become three independent corpus categories: the implementation enables comparison, and the conceptual move is instantiated and examined through that comparison. The two questions concern intended phone use or intended activity after phone use. Active typing and passive mental reflection form a second design factor.

The actual sequence is Introduction; Background and Related Work; Methodology (implementation, study design, metrics, participants); Results (quantitative and qualitative); Limitations; Discussion and Future Work; Conclusion; acknowledgments; references. Limitations precede the interpretive discussion. The design is 2×2 mixed: question between participants, response mode within participants with counterbalanced order. Twenty-eight participants used the app for two weeks, with pre-intervention and post-removal week data making a four-week measurement frame. Neither duration should replace the other in an abstract summary.

The abstract begins with the intervention and factors, specifies study design and duration, separates question effects from response-mode effects, adds contextual preferences, and states discussion scope. Introduction p 1819 develops the problem, earlier regulation approaches and conflicting findings, their device-centered scope, the physical-world gap, a working mindfulness definition, the intervention, two RQs, study, results, and contributions. This is close to a gap-to-question spine, but its definition and detailed design rationale occupy substantial argument space.

Related work differentiates digital wellbeing from simply reducing all use, explains mindfulness and habitual checking, and organizes interventions by when and how they act. The comparison with MyTime is particularly useful: acknowledging related aspirations while specifying the missing direct comparison avoids pretending that prior work never considered nonphone activities. The gap is a design-and-evidence distinction, not a claim of wholly untouched territory.

## Evidence and limits

Participants copied OS-recorded screen time and unlock counts into surveys; these are not estimated durations, but neither are they researcher-collected raw telemetry. Absentminded use is measured separately with SUQ-A. Open-ended responses contextualize reflection, friction, annoyance, guilt, surveillance feelings, and control. Quantitative nonsignificance between response modes is compatible with different preferences and mechanisms; it does not prove identical experiences or equivalence.

The activity question is associated with lower use measures than the intention question, while removal is followed by rebound. Claims about enduring self-regulation remain participant expectations and future possibilities. The short deployment, sample, reporting channel, and no separate untreated concurrent group limit causal breadth and durability. Qualitative convergence does not eliminate sampling bias. The manuscript's Table 1 subgroup gender counts do not sum to its stated subgroup Ns; preference totals also require care. Do not manufacture reconciled demographics or borrow its strongest causal phrasing without checking analysis and baseline comparisons.

## Two micro-examples

**p 1827/PDF 10, §6.1, “Our two questions were” (four sentences).** S 1 restates the design contrast; S 2 reports perceived awareness for the intention question; S 3 contrasts the absence of detected quantitative behavior change; S 4 infers an additional intervention requirement. The preceding paragraph positions the study against prior work; the next develops activity-question findings. This is topical setup followed by mixed-evidence contrast, rather than conclusion-first prose. The fourth sentence is stronger than the directly observed null result and should be treated as an interpretive recommendation, not proof that the approach can never work.

**p 1828/PDF 11, §6.2, “Our quantitative results showed” (six sentences).** S 1 interprets the passive-mode outcome with prior work; S 2 contrasts differentiated qualitative preference; S 3 gives the burden-versus-barrier split; S 4 explains passive ease and skipping; S 5 proposes choice or adaptive switching; S 6 proposes answer reuse. The preceding paragraph differentiates goals served by the two questions; the next introduces contextual automation and its difficulty. This is finding-first integration that turns disagreement into configurable design options. The proposed version pictured nearby is future design, not an evaluated condition.

**Transfer.** Distinguish deployment from measurement duration, behavioral quantity from reflective meaning, and intended nonjudgmental design from experienced judgment. Use divergent evidence to explain when configurations might fit; preserve rebound and burden rather than writing an unqualified wellbeing success story.
