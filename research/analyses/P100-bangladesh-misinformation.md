# P100 — Responsibility gaps across a misinformation ecology

**Source and reading.** Haque, Yousuf, Alam, Saha, Ahmed, and Hassan, *Combating Misinformation in Bangladesh: Roles and Responsibilities as Perceived by Journalists, Fact-checkers, and Users*, PACMHCI 4(CSCW 2), 2020, article 130, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3415201). Analyzed the complete substantive text and interview appendices of [arXiv v 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.12841), August 27, 2020, 32 pages. Some malformed punctuation is present in the rendered manuscript itself. Selected for substantive multi-stakeholder integration and the relation between professional responsibility, infrastructure, and public expectations. No award claimed. Single-analyst coding; two whole-paragraph boundaries checked in rendered pages.

## Contribution and section logic

This is primarily an empirical account of an institutional coordination problem, using mixed evidence. Seven fact-checker interviews, 12 newspaper journalist interviews, 29 user interviews, and 500 online survey responses support the account. Neither adding percentages nor proposing tools makes the contribution an artifact or statistical-model contribution. The claim becomes interesting through a mismatch: users expect verification from organizations whose responsibilities, resources, access, and political safety do not match that expectation.

Actual structure: Introduction; Related Work (global challenge, approaches, journalistic values); ICT, Journalism, and Freedom of the Press in Bangladesh; Methodology; Findings (mainstream media, fact-checkers, general users); Discussion (local responses, broader implications, limitations); acknowledgments; references; interview-protocol appendix. There is no separately titled conclusion. The context section explains institutional constraints before readers evaluate reported practices. Findings organized by stakeholder lead to discussion organized by coordination and intervention opportunities: the two organizational schemes perform different work.

The abstract moves from disciplinary interest and geographic limitation through local stakes, methods, a three-party mismatch, disciplinary connections, and proposed responses. Introduction pp 2–3 similarly moves from general problem to concrete Bangladesh incidents, neighboring cases and their different capacities, methods, and three contribution bullets. It does not need a stand-alone RQ list. Related work pp 3–7 first maps variation, then intervention families, then contested professional values. Its strongest positioning move identifies prerequisites of computational solutions—accessible information, infrastructure, and press freedom—that cannot be assumed locally. Parts are historically enumerative; successful publication does not make every list-like passage an ideal synthesis model.

## Claim–evidence–warrant–limit

Methods explain why user interviews supplemented an online survey disproportionately reaching educated young people. The useful warrant is complementary access and richer accounts, not that convenience samples become representative when combined. Findings preserve stakeholder boundaries before comparison: journalists distinguish verifying their own publications from policing the whole web; independent checkers describe constraints; users report strategies and responsibility expectations. Quotations support interpreted practices, while percentages describe survey responses. Neither directly measures actual misinformation prevalence or successful verification behavior.

Discussion proposes collaboration, press freedom, information access, faith-based engagement, and language resources. These are reasoned implications rather than evaluated remedies. Transfer rests on specified contextual similarities, with nationally unrepresentative samples explicitly acknowledged. Numeric reporting needs care: p 17's approximate 400 news-website respondents paired with 25.13% cannot be read as a straightforward percentage of 500; Table 3 also normalizes responses differently from overall multiple-selection percentages. A drafting skill should require denominator labels, not reproduce these ambiguities.

## Two sentence-flow observations

**p 8, §4, “This research aims to” (nine sentences).** S 1 specifies the knowledge question; S 2 names the multi-stakeholder approach; S 3 identifies mixed evidence; S 4 assigns interviews to qualitative evidence; S 5 assigns survey to quantitative evidence; S 6 identifies the survey's demographic concentration; S 7 explains the interview response to that coverage problem; S 8 adds depth as a second rationale; S 9 announces methodological details. The preceding paragraph closes the press-freedom context, while the next explains instrument construction. This is a purpose-first topical opening, not a conclusion-first result paragraph. The general-to-specific movement makes methodological combination intelligible.

**p 22, §6.1.5, “We observe that most” (five sentences).** S 1 names prerequisites of automated fact-checking; S 2 proposes Bengali linguistic resources; S 3 connects language accessibility to established CSCW work; S 4 adds a Global South adaptation tradition; S 5 positions this paper's call within those traditions. The preceding paragraph discusses access to information and policy; the next section considers broader transfer. This begins with a diagnostic premise, then recommendation and literature warrant. It does not demonstrate that the recommended resources would solve political constraints.

**Reusable lesson and exception.** Integrate methods at the level of what each can reveal, then integrate stakeholders around a shared coordination question. A longer contextual section and stakeholder-specific findings are defensible when later synthesis needs them. Avoid turning mixed methods into compulsory triangulation language, or treating every design recommendation as a second independently established contribution.
