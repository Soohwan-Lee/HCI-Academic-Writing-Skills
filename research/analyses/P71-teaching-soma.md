# P71 — Teaching soma design through curriculum and reflective critique

## Provenance and reading

Tsaknaki, Balaam, Ståhl, Sanches, Windlin, Karpashevich, and Höök, *Teaching Soma Design*, DIS 2019, DOI 10.1145/3322276.3322327. Read the full main text and conclusion-like recommendations in the [Zenodo-hosted proceedings copy](https://zenodo.org/records/3577223/files/Tsaknaki%20et%20al.%20-%202019%20-%20Teaching%20Soma%20Design.pdf?download=1), pp. 1237–1249. The first page verifies the bibliographic identity and KTH/RISE SICS affiliations. The [first author's research page](https://vasilikitsaknaki.com/research) reports a best-paper nomination; this is not independently verified as an official award or honorable mention and must not be relabeled. Single-analyst, version-dependent coding selects the paper for its explicit distinction between teaching process, concept potential, and fully realized interaction.

## Actual structure and opening

Introduction → Background (bodily engagement; sociodigital material) → Soma Design Course: Content and Methods (week-by-week activities) → Four Design Concepts, each followed by analysis → Lessons Learnt on How to Teach Soma Design → Adapting How We Teach Soma Design → acknowledgments/references. The ending develops concrete curriculum changes rather than a separate generic Conclusion heading. Descriptive design passages and evaluative passages are visibly differentiated.

The abstract starts directly with the seven-week curriculum, then presents both promising design possibilities and detailed student difficulties before offering recommendations and a toolkit. The introduction pp. 1237–1238 frames a mismatch between established HCI teaching and bodily/material interaction, introduces soma design as a possible response, and situates the challenge in one technical university's existing pedagogy. The research question concerns devising curriculum and teaching tactics; it is not an experiment comparing all design education approaches. A closing roadmap states the 28-student course, analysis of four of nine concepts, and intended outputs.

Background connects the aesthetic and somatic turns to educational practice. It discusses why bodily experience matters, what counts as material in soma design, and why simply invoking embodiment is insufficient. Existing approaches become resources for particular exercises: somaesthetic expertise, material exploration, and making familiar activities strange. This develops the rationale behind the curriculum, rather than postponing theory until an unconnected discussion section.

## Contribution, evidence, and warrant

The primary contribution is pedagogical and methodological knowledge from a situated course. Curriculum description, student pictorial accounts, subsequent discussions, and teachers' firsthand experience of demonstrated concepts provide the evidence. The four cases are purposively selected to reveal strengths and problems; they are not a random sample of student performance. The authors explicitly say that incomplete prototypes permit judgment of somaesthetic potential, not complete experiences in use.

Inhubator shows continuity from embodied exercises but also overloading of senses; sBlender illustrates how an evocative experience can become reduced to conventional speed control. These contrasts support claims about teaching difficulties and possible revisions. Teachers acknowledge what they failed to communicate, making instructional decisions part of the explanation. Statements that students opened an unusually rich design space are interpretive assessments against local teaching experience, not causal comparative results. The later toolkit is a response to observed limitations, with brief workshop observations; the paper does not become primarily a toolkit benchmark. The distinction between slowly developing sensitivity and designing slow user experiences is especially important: a process quality must not automatically become a product requirement.

## Two rendered paragraph micro-analyses

PDF p. 6 / printed p. 1242, Analysis of Inhubator, opening **“But where the design possibly”**, contains four sentences. S1 qualifies previous praise with a possible overemphasis. S2 connects that interpretation to the teaching message. S3 describes the resulting sensory overload. S4 accepts instructional responsibility and articulates the missing distinction between multiple stimuli and a coherent experience. The preceding paragraph identifies promising bodily qualities; the following considers slowing down as another potentially literalized teaching tactic. This is qualified critique-first writing whose causal interpretation is grounded in the teachers' account, not an isolated objective score.

PDF p. 8 / printed p. 1244, Shifting from Rationalizing to Experiencing, opening **“This said, to properly do”**, has three sentences. S1 states a prerequisite of developing sensitivity. S2 explains that this demands time. S3 bounds what the short course can provide. The preceding paragraph advocates repeated enactment and careful attention before committing to a concept; the following subsection turns to material and form-factor constraints. This limitation paragraph prevents an instructional recommendation from implying that one course completes the proposed training.

## Transfer and exceptions

For reflective pedagogy papers, disclose the teaching setting, evidence selection, researchers' instructional role, and what student artifacts can actually demonstrate. Separate intended experience from observed prototype behavior. A reusable writing skill can model explicit self-critique and theory-to-exercise links without prescribing soma methods or turning a contextual curriculum into a universal educational formula.
