## Contribution and non-linear architecture

The paper contributes a reusable card-toolkit extension, an account of translating theory into tools, and heuristics for extending existing tools. Resource and design-method knowledge are co-primary; reducing the paper to either a card release or an effectiveness experiment loses its contract. Research and classroom cases demonstrate use, while the design process supports methodological reflection.

After the abstract, “How to Read” offers multiple routes through nine parts: visual landscape; conceptual landscape; concept into tool; generating visual concepts; generating action prompts; research case; educational case; heuristics; reflection. A circular map groups alternative reading pathways. There is no conventional Introduction–Related Work–Methods–Results progression. Images, handwritten process traces, card faces, annotated scenarios, and student artifacts carry the argument alongside prose. This is a substantive counterexample to applying an 8,000-word sectional ratio to every HCI paper form.

## Framing and scholarly positioning

The abstract (p. 557) identifies the theory–practice gap, the difficulty of constructing bridging tools, and three contributions. Page 558 repeats these as navigational commitments. The early conceptual landscape (p. 560) narrows multi-lifespan complexity into dimensions that can support action. Part 3 (p. 561) situates the extension within the original Envisioning Cards and reports the historical deck composition; current product counts should not replace the analyzed version.

Related work is distributed by argumentative purpose. Design-card precedents justify a familiar form; multi-lifespan theory supplies themes; value sensitive design explains conceptual commitments. Part 8 (pp. 566–567) confronts the assumption that contribution always means a wholly new tool: extending an existing resource can incorporate emerging theory. This is an argument for extensibility, not merely a list of related toolkits.

## Claim, support, warrant, limits

Part 4 shows that unsuccessful image choices exposed conceptual problems and led to splitting a theme. Part 5 makes reflection actionable through prompts that produce diagrams, sketches, lists, scenarios, or timelines. The warrant for theory-to-tool translation is traceability among concepts, representational decisions, and possible actions. The research case uses a hypothetical technology, Sleeve; the classroom case reports a ninety-minute studio. These are illustrative cases, not controlled effectiveness evidence or long-term deployment. No conventional limitations section supplies participant-level or comparative evaluation details. Transfer should therefore preserve the modest demonstrative warrant while making the tool's reuse conditions explicit.

The preceding left caption connects multigenerational theory to a provisional theme; the following right caption names the two revised concepts. Images above and finished cards below supply an additional evidential sequence. This paragraph begins with a process event, not a deductive conclusion. Its useful move is making a failed representation explain theoretical revision.

**p. 567, Part 8, Consider Form, opening “Each card follows” (five sentences).** S1 specifies recurring components. S2 derives the combination of repeatability and flexibility. S3 warns against excessive rigidity; S4 against excessive permissiveness. S5 states the interoperability consequence and points back to the case. The page-level introductory paragraph frames three heuristics. Adjacent Focus and Granularity columns supply parallel considerations rather than conventional sequential paragraphs; the next page integrates concepts, tools, and action. A descriptive opening supports a bounded principle through paired failure conditions, rather than announcing the entire conclusion in sentence one.

## Transfer conditions

For reusable design resources, show construction choices, an actual example of use, and how another practitioner can adapt the resource. A pictorial can make process evidence spatially inspectable; its captions and annotations should not be treated as decorative leftovers. Heuristics derived from a design account require scope statements. Do not turn these examples into universal card-design laws or replace their visual argument with generic prose sections.
