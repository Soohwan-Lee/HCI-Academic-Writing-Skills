## Structure and rhetoric

The actual sequence is Introduction → Postphenomenology (including its use in HCI) → Investigations of the Table-Non-Table and Tilting Bowl: Introductory Examples → Our Methodological Approach → Annotated Portfolio of Postphenomenological Commitments in Research Products → Discussion → Conclusion. The portfolio repeats a three-part conceptual organization: empirical bases, structures of relationships, and technological mediation. Definitions, prior examples, artifact-specific interpretations, and concluding remarks recur within these categories.

The abstract states the philosophical framing, explains the annotated-portfolio approach, names three commitments, identifies six inquiries, and gives conceptual and methodological implications. Introduction pp. 459–460 first questions utility-focused accounts, positions postphenomenology as a useful alternative, explains its mutual relationship with RtD, then motivates an annotated portfolio through two earlier projects. It does not pretend the framing preceded every project. The table-non-table account explicitly describes years of reframing before the authors understood it in these terms.

There is no freestanding Related Work section. The Postphenomenology section teaches the vocabulary and argues that generative use in design remains underdeveloped despite useful analytical applications. The introductory examples make that possibility concrete. Methodological positioning then explains why actual, finished, independently operating research products and counterfactual artifacts are relevant. This progression resolves a comprehension dependency: the reader needs the philosophical lens before understanding the selection and annotation logic.

## Claim and warrant

The claim is that making and studying research products can constitute experimental postphenomenological inquiry. Support consists of reinterpretations of published cases, not a new comparative user experiment. Selection begins with over thirty artifacts, narrows by material-speculation/research-product commitments and available accounts of real-world experience, and seeks varied sources. The portfolio includes Greenscreen Dress, Obscura 1C, Indoor Weather Stations, Morse Things, Photobox, and Datacatcher. Evidence is asymmetric: it includes autobiographical encounters, deployments, participant reports, and descriptions of distribution without follow-up. The paper does not convert all of these into equivalent behavioral measurements.

The warrant connects concrete encounters to relational structures and then to how technology shapes experiences and worlds. Discussion p. 468 identifies two extensions: researchers craft the object of inquiry and co-construct its context and encounters. That gives a reason for the methodological argument beyond labeling artifacts with philosophical terms. Limits include purposive selection, interpretive dependence on published accounts, and nonexhaustive relationship categories. The authors also acknowledge that the abstract concepts led to a more textual portfolio than anticipated; its format is an outcome of this argument, not a claim that all portfolios should be text-heavy.

## Transfer and exception

For conceptual synthesis, expose the selection rationale and show each abstraction through instances before widening its significance. Repeated conceptual substructures can help readers compare cases without author-by-author cataloguing. Preserve the distinction between original observations and reinterpretation of others’ reports. Do not present this purposive annotated portfolio as an exhaustive systematic review, use six cases as prevalence evidence, or infer that a theoretical lens must have been present at project inception. Its dialogic relation between artifact and theory is transferable; its particular ontology is a commitment requiring justification.
