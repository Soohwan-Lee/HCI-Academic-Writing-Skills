## Architecture and opening moves

Introduction, including definition and motivation subsections → Experience Prototyping in Practice → Understanding Existing User Experiences → Exploring and Evaluating Design Ideas → Communicating Ideas → Conclusions and Next Steps, including Establishing an Attitude and What We Don't Know. Case examples sit within the three purposes, not in an undifferentiated catalog. The final open questions are substantial argumentative work rather than an obligatory sentence about future research.

The short abstract defines the approach through participants' firsthand engagement, identifies commercial examples as the evidence, and previews the three activities. Introduction p. 424 describes a practical challenge, defines prototypes, credits existing prototyping research, and proposes an experiential extension. It does not manufacture an absence of prior work: the conclusion explicitly acknowledges that designers have long adapted methods and materials. The contribution is conscious articulation and organization of practice. Its definition moves from experience as contextual and dynamic to prototypes that allow active participation, distinguishing this paper's focus from passive demonstrations without dismissing their value.

Related work provides distinctions—role, appearance/feel, implementation, fidelity, audience, participation—that clarify what experience prototyping includes and extends. Subsequent examples return to these dimensions through material decisions and context. The practical question supplies the progression; there is no formal RQ list, separately labeled literature review, or statistical hypothesis.

## Contribution and evidence warrant

The knowledge object is a design approach supported by practitioner cases and reflective analysis. The patient pager simulation, remotely operated vehicle role-play, train journey, game controller props, airplane layout, television control, children's communicator, digital camera, and intimate communication prototype each illustrate a different relationship between design questions and experiential representation. The examples are neither a systematic sample nor controlled comparative evaluations.

Useful case writing identifies what was simulated, what was omitted, who participated, and what decision became possible. In the ROV example, feedback from a retired pilot enriches and checks the team's simulation. In the camera example, a convincing prototype also has an unwanted consequence: clients treat the design as finished. This counterexample makes persuasion a risk as well as a benefit. Some assertions about recognizing a good experience quickly are stronger than the reported evidence warrants; a derivative skill should not convert them into evaluation rules.

The closing discussion explicitly limits empathy: designers cannot become other people through simulation. Prototypes remain partial, and observational, participatory, and evaluative methods contribute complementary perspectives. The authors also question whether performance for an audience may displace actual experiencing. These concessions are essential to the method's scope, not reasons to erase the experiential contribution.

## Two rendered paragraph micro-analyses

PDF p. 2 / printed p. 425, motivation subsection, opening **“The tools we use to”**, contains four sentences. S1 states that design tools shape thinking. S2 specifies how tools enable and constrain solutions and imagination. S3 grounds that claim in practitioners' observations of drafting, modeling, and new materials. S4 connects the premise to experience prototyping. The preceding paragraph discusses personal experience and empathy; the following section introduces the three practice categories. The opening is a general claim, while the final sentence supplies the particular implication. The examples warrant a practitioner rationale, not a quantified causal effect.

PDF p. 9 / printed p. 432, final main-text paragraph, opening **“Finally, we come back”**, has five sentences. S1 restores the complexity of experience. S2 states the limit of designer prediction. S3 maintains the importance of experiential design work despite that limit. S4 defines the approach's contribution while retaining its partiality. S5 calls for deliberate experimentation. The preceding paragraph explains selecting and framing simulated elements; acknowledgments follow. This conclusion develops through qualification and recovery, rather than repeating an unbounded success claim.

## Transfer conditions

For a method articulated from professional practice, organize examples by what each enables researchers or designers to learn. Connect a prototype's fidelity to its question and audience, not to an assumed hierarchy of realism. Show informative failures alongside productive cases. The writing pattern supports methodological articulation; it cannot substitute for user evidence when the manuscript claims effects on a target population.
