# P95 — Mobile information needs beyond search logs

Source: Sohn, Li, Griswold, and Hollan, *A Diary Study of Mobile Information Needs*, CHI 2008. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357125). Complete ten-page [author-hosted conference manuscript](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~tsohn/pdf/tsohn-mobileneeds-chi08.pdf) read. It carries CHI 2008 identification and local pages 1–10; the similarly titled 2007 UC technical report is a distinct version. Single-analyst coding; no award claimed.

## Argument and contribution

The knowledge contribution is an empirical characterization of mobile information needs, the circumstances prompting them, and strategies for satisfying or postponing them. Twenty participants produced 421 diary entries during two weeks, supplemented by three interviews each. Descriptive category counts organize the qualitative material; they are not population estimates. The snippet-based SMS diary is an adapted data-collection method, not the paper's primary invention. Design suggestions are implications of observed difficulties, not evidence that a new predictive system works.

The actual sequence is Introduction; Methods (Participants, Procedure); Results and Discussion (need taxonomy, decisions about timing, methods of access, reasons for delay or abandonment, mobile-internet differences, contextual prompts, information sources); Design Implications; Conclusion; acknowledgments and references. There is no freestanding Related Work section or separate Limitations heading. Prior literature is instead placed where it justifies framing or methodological choices.

The abstract moves from mobility's contextual constraints to the need for understanding, the diary method, observed strategic diversity, and design implications. The introduction starts with recognizable information episodes, describes mobile-device access and interaction constraints, challenges desktop-to-mobile transplantation, then distinguishes recorded searches from underlying needs. Search logs miss unexpressed needs and exclude people without capable devices. The study and its findings follow this sampling-and-construct gap. Methods similarly compares experience sampling, diaries, voice capture, and snippet approaches according to burden and recall, turning related work into a rationale for the chosen procedure.

## Claims, warrants, and cautions

The strongest claim is that connectivity alone does not explain whether mobile information needs are satisfied. Diaries and interview accounts show competing attention, urgency, social appropriateness, monetary costs, and access to people or physical places. Distinguishing immediate, later, and never-addressed needs provides the analytical warrant for studying postponement and contextual delivery. Five participants had mobile internet; their experiences complicate the nonusers' expectation that connectivity would solve most needs. This is a situated comparison, not a causal estimate of adopting mobile internet.

Recruitment required texting experience, reporting depended on participants recognizing and recording needs, and occupation and activity distributions shape category frequencies. The authors note that predictable needs may go unreported and infrequent categories can still be important. A diary request can also intersect with unsafe driving practices, described rather than validated. All interpretations remain historical to these devices and services. The analyzed version contains a numerical inconsistency: the information-source discussion gives 24% solely personal plus 14% indirect access, whereas the implications restatement prints 28% plus 14% alongside a 38% total. Preserve that discrepancy rather than silently treating the restatement as clean measurement.

## Two visually checked paragraph analyses

**PDF p.9, information sources, “Public data was the.”** Complete five-sentence paragraph in the left column. S1 states the largest source category with its proportion; S2 relates this to participants' internet associations; S3 contrasts the dominance of public data with the importance of personal data; S4 distinguishes personal-only access from a personal-to-public retrieval strategy using examples; S5 interprets that strategy as both necessary and interactionally costly. The preceding paragraph defines the source categories; the next isolates physical-only information. This is conclusion-first at the descriptive level, with a later inferential turn. It teaches how to make category counts serve a process explanation rather than merely rank categories.

**PDF p.9, Design Implications, “Designing such a system.”** Complete three-sentence paragraph in the right column. S1 qualifies the proposed context-aware system through misclassification and nuisance; S2 extends the concern to unwanted personalization, spam, and privacy infrastructure; S3 makes resolving these issues a condition of improving access. The preceding paragraph synthesizes an envisioned design space; the following reports participant responses to that vision. This is a constraint-first paragraph, not an unsupported negative aside. Its placement prevents the design proposal from becoming an inevitable solution before user ambivalence is introduced.

## Reusable choices

Define the phenomenon beyond what an available log happens to record. Separate occurrence frequency from situational importance. Let methods literature explain why data capture fits the phenomenon. Integrate findings and discussion when individual cases need immediate interpretation, while retaining an explicit boundary between observed behavior and proposed technical response. This selection adds a diary-based precedent from UCSD with a concrete sampling argument; institutional reputation alone is not evidence of a writing rule.
