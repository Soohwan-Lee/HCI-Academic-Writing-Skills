# P41 — Design in the HCI Classroom

**Source and scope.** Wilcox, DiSalvo, Henneman, and Wang, DIS 2019, *Design in the HCI Classroom: Setting a Research Agenda*, DOI 10.1145/3322276.3322381. Author-hosted proceedings-formatted PDF. Discovery followed established Georgia Tech education/design scholars; award status remains unverified. Full body read; PDF p. 9 visually checked. This analysis is a single coder's reading of one version.

**Contribution and support.** Primary contribution is a descriptive empirical characterization of design instruction, supporting a pedagogical research agenda. Evidence combines thematic analysis of 14 instructional books with an international survey of 61 HCI instructors, descriptive statistics, and collaborative thematic analysis of open responses. The article reviews prior scholarship too, but it is not a systematic literature review with exhaustive retrieval claims. No instructional intervention is evaluated. The proposed agenda is an independently articulated synthesis of empirical and conceptual tensions, not a demonstrated improvement in student learning.

**Actual structure.** Introduction → Related Work (education history, education research, design practices, design in HCI education) → Methods (instructional books; survey design, participants, analysis) → Findings on books → HCI Education Survey (course characteristics, instruction and goals) → Discussion (distinctive approaches and education-research implications) → Conclusion. The separate survey heading continues the empirical reporting rather than introducing an unrelated study. The discussion organizes the agenda around recognizing distinctive approaches, teaching formal iterative design, and teaching creative design. There is no separately labeled limitations section; scope restrictions occur prominently in the introduction and methods footnotes.

**Abstract and introduction.** The abstract moves from disciplinary and professional demand to the aim of distilling instruction, then sources, contrasts/tensions, and the case for pedagogy research. Introduction pp. 1–2 makes design's centrality conflict with disciplinary fragmentation, states the research question, bounds the focus to ACM-associated computing and information programs, and locates both community efforts and the authors' teaching experience. It then previews variability, explicitly says variability itself is not bad, distinguishes formal iterative and creative design, identifies missing pedagogical guidance, and outlines the investigation. Results previews therefore participate in constructing the problem; they are not confined to a final contributions bullet list.

**Related-work positioning.** The historical subsection establishes that design has long been curricular rather than newly discovered. Education research reveals existing work concentrated on techniques and local methods; design-practice theory provides a useful distinction that has not been adequately examined as pedagogy. Literature on studio teaching then complicates transplantation into computing classrooms. The synthesis argues for studying relationships among disciplinary assumptions, learning goals, institutional constraints, and instruction. Its gap is not simply a lack of studies but insufficient guidance about selecting and integrating approaches under specific conditions.

**Claim–evidence–warrant–limit.** Book classifications and instructor responses jointly support a mismatch between declared creative goals and the stronger representation of formal iterative methods. Convergence across sources provides a descriptive warrant, while the discussion carefully defines formal iterative design instead of equating it uncritically with engineering. It cannot establish what instructors actually do in classrooms or what students learn. Amazon rankings and suitability judgments delimit the books; network/conference recruitment and missing geographical regions delimit the survey. The authors themselves call for observation, syllabi analysis, interviews, and learning assessments. Survey percentages describe respondents, not global HCI education. Some broad discussion assertions about classroom feasibility are agenda-setting arguments rather than directly tested thresholds.

**Paragraph micro-analysis.** Discussion, PDF p. 9, opening locator “Indeed, design is central”: S1 restates disciplinary centrality; S2 grounds that centrality in surveyed course naming; S3 contrasts it with missing creative/theoretical content; S4 adds inconsistency in instructors' expressed alignment; S5 interprets the discrepancy as a difference between intended and taught approaches. Relations: background premise → empirical support → contrast → further evidence → interpretive synthesis. This is topical/premise-first, not conclusion-first: the consequential mismatch emerges last. The preceding paragraph describes textbook dominance of formal iteration; the following paragraph rejects privileging one approach and presents three research directions. This sequence turns a mismatch into an agenda without declaring one pedagogy inherently superior.

**Writing lesson and exception.** State scope early when a title could imply universal coverage. Use a distinction from prior theory as a lens, then define where the empirical material requires adapting it. An agenda paper can earn its directions through multiple modest evidence sources; it need not pretend the agenda has already been validated. Conversely, clear organization and an established author team do not justify copying its strong pedagogical claims as proven general rules. No numerical prose norms are inferred here.
