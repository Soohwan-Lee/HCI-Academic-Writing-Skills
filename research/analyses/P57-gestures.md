# P57 — User-Defined Gestures for Surface Computing

Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson, CHI 2009. University of Washington and Microsoft
Research. [Institution-hosted PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2009/04/SurfaceGestures_CHI2009.pdf).
Full text read; PDF p. 9 rendered for both micro examples. Award unverified.

## Contribution and architecture
This is a hybrid empirical/design-resource paper. The authors explicitly claim
characterization and taxonomy, a gesture set, mental-model insights, and design
implications. Elicitation is the method and is credited to earlier work; this
paper should not be coded as inventing elicitation simply because the procedure
became influential. Its reusable gesture vocabulary and characterization each
have identifiable outputs. The method description explains how those outputs
were constructed.

Order: Introduction → Related Work → Developing a User-Defined Gesture Set →
Results (classification, vocabulary construction, performance/preferences,
mental-model observations) → Discussion (designer comparison, technology,
interfaces, limitations/next steps) → Conclusion. Results mix quantitative and
qualitative evidence according to the object being explained rather than making
one strand an unconnected appendix. The abstract contrasts designer-selected
vocabularies with elicited behavior, states the procedure and corpus, previews
findings, enumerates outputs and states intended design use.

The introduction makes the freedom of surface gestures into a design problem,
identifies the limits of recognition-driven choices, asks a cluster of concrete
questions, introduces elicitation and mixed observations, then names contributions.
Its novelty rhetoric must be read alongside Related Work, which acknowledges
prior user-input elicitation. A skill should keep this local scope instead of
turning a surface-specific claim into the first use of user participation.

## Related work and synthesis
The review groups gesture classification, elicitation, and existing systems.
These strands justify respectively the need for an appropriate vocabulary, the
chosen procedure, and a contrast with designer-defined gestures. Prior taxonomies
are limited by what they classify, not dismissed as incorrect. Earlier prompts
are considered as possible sources of bias. Later findings return to specific
system assumptions about fingers and gestures. The literature therefore supplies
both intellectual ancestry and design assumptions to revisit.

The study deliberately omits recognition feedback to observe unrevised behavior.
That enables one kind of inquiry while excluding evidence about an operational
recognizer. Agreement scores guide conflict resolution; the resulting vocabulary
is not a list of every gesture observed. Observed agreement and self-ratings are
not equivalent to independently tested guessability, recognition accuracy or
long-term learnability. The paper explicitly leaves new-participant validation
and recognizer implementation for future work. Its strong design recommendations
should retain boundaries concerning the particular participants, prompts, culture
and historical interface experience. Do not repeat dated population descriptions
or universalize the paper's normative wording.

## Micro-analysis A: a design implication with a counterweight
PDF p. 9, Implications for Surface Technology opening, locator “Many of the gestures”.
Five sentences, visually checked. S1 signals technological relevance. S2 connects
the prevalence of physical gestures to a candidate recognition approach. S3 gives
one observed expectation. S4 adds another concrete behavior. S5 qualifies the
proposal using the presence of other gesture classes. Flow: **implication →
pattern → examples → boundary**. The previous discussion compares user/designer
vocabularies; the next paragraph considers sensing geometry. The final sentence
prevents a subset of observations from justifying a universal technical solution.

## Micro-analysis B: expose what remains unvalidated
Same page, Limitations and Next Steps, final paragraph, locator “An important next
step”. Four sentences, counting the parenthesized sentence. S1 names validation
as unfinished work. S2 proposes a new-participant comparison. S3 explains that it
reverses the elicitation mapping. S4 separates implementation and recognition
measurement as a later step. Flow: **open claim → proposed test → inferential
rationale → subsequent technical test**. The preceding paragraph describes
context and procedure limitations; the next section concludes. An editor must
preserve the future tense and must not rewrite this plan as a completed study.

## Transfer and exceptions
When a paper contributes both empirical understanding and a reusable design
output, show how analysis constructs that output and where its validation stops.
Related-work categories can map to distinct intellectual needs. Integrate mixed
observations when they explain the same object, while keeping their respective
warrants visible. Do not infer that every taxonomy is a theory contribution or
that elicitation agreement proves usability. These distinctions matter more than
forcing a hybrid paper into one venue-specific section template.
