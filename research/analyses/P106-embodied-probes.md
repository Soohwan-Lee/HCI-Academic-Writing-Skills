# P106 — Embodied co-design as a means of self-disclosure

**Source and scope.** Sun, Claisse, Zhang, Wu, Yuan, and Wang, *Conversations With The Stressed Body: Facilitating Stress Self-Disclosure Among Adolescent Girls Through An Embodied Approach*, DIS 2025, pp 3421–3439, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3715336.3735707). Read the complete substantive text and design-response appendix of the [author-hosted publisher PDF](https://www.runhuazhang.com/pubs/pdfs/dis-25.pdf): 20 PDF pages including an ACM cover. Selected for explicit pilot-to-main iteration, youth-centered embodied methods, and a distinction between design activity as inquiry and prototype effectiveness. No award claimed. Single-analyst coding with two rendered complete-paragraph checks.

## Contribution, method, architecture

The primary knowledge is how embodied workshop practices support stress-related disclosure and what participants disclose. The probes and participant prototypes provide the inquiry setting and design material; three working prototypes do not establish a clinically effective stress-management system. A six-participant pilot with ages 18–19 informs a two-day main workshop with nine girls aged 15–18 in Shanghai. Follow-up interviews occur one week later. These stages form an iterative design-and-inquiry argument rather than two independent confirmations of the same effect.

Actual sequence: Introduction; Related Works; Overall Methods (overview, recruitment ethics, positionality); Pilot Workshop (protocol, findings); Embodied Probes Workshop (participants, design, probes, analysis); Findings organized by two RQs; Discussion (approach, implications, limitations); Conclusion; acknowledgments; references; participant-design appendix. Pilot findings explicitly become three design considerations, and the main protocol describes their implementation. The method detail serves reproducibility and interpretive context; it is not merely an inventory of creative activities.

The abstract moves from adolescent stress to an embodied-disclosure gap, workshop/probe approach, participants and activities, insights, and design contribution. Introduction pp 3421–3422 narrows from girls' contextual pressures through self-disclosure, limitations of textual/verbal expression, bodily experience, two workshop stages, RQs, and contributions. Related work follows that same dependency: population/context, disclosure interventions, then soma design as methodological warrant. It acknowledges relevant gender-specific precedents rather than asserting that girls' bodily experiences have never been studied. The distinction is how embodiment supports stress disclosure in this setting.

## Claim–evidence–warrant–limit

Audio, interviews, drawings, sculptures, and prototypes enter the qualitative analysis together with participants' explanations. The first finding set traces awareness, externalization, and expressive making; the second characterizes disclosed symptoms, sources, and coping strategies. Quotations name their activity context, enabling readers to distinguish a follow-up interpretation from an in-workshop account. The codebook connects RQs to themes; counts of coded experiences are not prevalence estimates.

The strongest warrant is an observable path from felt sensation through material representation and conversation to disclosed experience. Design justification itself becomes another occasion for disclosure. Claims that workshop components caused clinical improvement, that a physiological symptom was medically caused by stress, or that embodiment outperforms verbal-only disclosure would exceed this evidence. The baseline stress questionnaire is mentioned but is not presented as a pre/post efficacy test. Safe-space arrangements are intentional and supported by some participant accounts; they are not a universal guarantee of safety.

Discussion uses the empirical process to suggest future tools and contextual design considerations. Limits include Shanghai recruitment, small sample, partial prior familiarity, self-reported mental-health screening, and a need to test other methods and settings. Planned extended deployment and China/UK comparison remain future work. Gendered and regional findings should preserve their sampling scope rather than become essential claims about all girls.

## Two paragraph micro-analyses

**Printed p 3424/PDF 5, §3.3, “The authors of this” (seven sentences).** S1 locates the research team; S2 connects relevant lived experience; S3 specifies shared cultural history; S4 explains its role in rapport; S5 describes embodied preparation; S6 identifies a concrete interpretive risk—overweighting academic stress; S7 describes ongoing critical discussion. The preceding paragraph explains consent and participant wellbeing; the next introduces the pilot. This is topical identification followed by methodological consequence. The useful move is linking identity to a possible analytic bias and an action, rather than presenting demographics alone as reflexivity.

**Printed p 3433/PDF 14, §7.1, “In addition to body” (nine sentences).** S1 introduces expressive making; S2 describes its customary design purpose; S3 contrasts its disclosure role here; S4 proposes intervention potential; S5 identifies design justification as an expressive occasion; S6 grounds design ideas in bodily exploration; S7 relates that exploration to articulated needs; S8 synthesizes the workshop's value; S9 proposes deliberately structuring future inquiry around this function. The preceding paragraph discusses embodied metaphors; the next generalizes the soma-design extension. This is activity-first, contrast-led reasoning. The proposed therapeutic role must remain distinct from the observed disclosure process.

**Transfer.** Show what a pilot changed, keep participants' meanings attached to artifacts, and organize findings around the knowledge questions. Co-design can be a disclosure method without requiring artifact evaluation or statistical reliability conventions inappropriate to its claims.
