# P93 — Stakeholder perceptions of automated team formation

Source: Jahanbakhsh et al., *You Want Me to Work with Who? Stakeholder Perceptions of Automated Team Formation in Project-based Courses*, CHI 2017. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3026011). Analyzed the complete author-hosted conference PDF; page locators below count PDF pages. This is single-analyst rhetorical coding, not an award-based prescription.

## Contribution and evidence architecture

The knowledge object is how students and instructors experience and configure algorithmic team formation in courses. CATME is an existing instrument and setting, not this paper's new artifact. Deployment in eight courses, 277 student surveys, and interviews with 13 instructors support a mixed empirical account. Counts and ratings characterize responses; qualitative categories explain agreement and disagreement. Recommendations are derived proposals, not experimentally demonstrated improvements. Selection is useful because stakeholder asymmetry, real configuration differences, and satisfaction-versus-performance distinctions make its writing richer than a simple acceptance study; no award is asserted.

The actual spine is Introduction; Related Work (tool studies, team composition, formation approaches); Team Formation Tool; Research Questions; Method; Results; Discussion and Future Work; Limitations; Conclusion. Results begin with actual configuration variation before student-question subsections and instructor perspectives. Explaining the tool before questions makes the decision environment intelligible; this is an exception to requiring every RQ inside the introduction.

The abstract moves from algorithmic uptake to missing experience evidence, describes both data sources, contrasts stakeholder findings, then announces recommendations. The introduction moves from educational teamwork and formation difficulty through algorithmic promise to the practical knowledge gap; it previews the field study, results, and two contributions. Related work distinguishes measured effectiveness from perceptions inferred through an absence of complaints. Team-composition findings then establish why selecting among plausible criteria is difficult. These literatures supply a decision problem, not just a vacancy for another evaluation.

## Claim, support, warrant, and limits

Students often appreciated rational criteria yet wanted input and explanations; instructors valued efficiency but disagreed about transparency. Quotations, categorized respondent counts, configuration tables, and ratings warrant this situated account. They do not establish that transparency increases team performance. The discussion explicitly leaves performance effects of proposed changes for future work. Course-specific satisfaction–grade correlations differ in direction, resisting a convenient equation of satisfaction with success.

Selection and setting matter: optional survey respondents came largely from computing courses in one institution; instructor configurations varied; some students appeared in multiple courses. Category counts concern respondents mentioning an issue, not prevalence in all enrolled students. The reported agreement check is part of this particular coding procedure, not a rule for all qualitative research. Claims about educational agency should retain the difference between student wishes, instructor rationales, and analyst recommendations.

## Two paragraph and sentence-flow observations

**PDF p.9, Discussion, opening “From our results.”** The complete six-sentence paragraph is visually verified in the right column. S1 announces a recommendation set grounded in findings; S2 supplies its first substantive proposal, involving students in criterion selection; S3 operationalizes that proposal through live polling or bounded input; S4 adds a ranking alternative; S5 connects rankings to weights; S6 distributes implementation between instructors and tool designers. This is a topical announcement followed by a recommendation, not an empirical conclusion in the opening sentence. The preceding paragraph summarizes weaknesses; the following paragraph changes to explanations for assigned teams. The progression translates one problem into feasible actions without claiming those actions were tested.

**PDF p.10, Discussion, opening “We used Spearman’s.”** The complete seven-sentence paragraph is visually verified in the left column. S1 introduces an analysis; S2 specifies its five-course scope; S3 explains missing-course exclusions; S4 summarizes inconsistent correlations; S5 supplies the values; S6 interprets satisfaction as only loosely connected to grades; S7 bounds that interpretation with a further research need. The opening is methodological, while the conclusion arrives after scope and evidence. The preceding paragraph raises configuration and framing uncertainties; the following paragraph broadens future work to composition versus team dynamics. This sequence legitimately places a supplementary analysis in discussion, although a new manuscript should make its analysis status clear rather than silently moving planned primary results there.

## Transferable writing decisions

Organize stakeholder comparisons around consequential disagreements rather than averaging them away. Keep recommendations adjacent to the empirical weakness they address. Let a table carry the full coding taxonomy while prose develops selected mechanisms and contradictions. Reuse the claim–scope–evidence–interpretation relationship, not this paper's precise section order or paragraph lengths.
