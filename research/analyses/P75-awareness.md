# P75 — Awareness and Coordination in Shared Workspaces

## Source and knowledge object

Dourish and Bellotti, CSCW 1992, DOI 10.1145/143457.143468, publisher pagination 107–114. The author-hosted manuscript has nine pages with its own numbered footer; locators below use that manuscript. Authors state Rank Xerox EuroPARC. Award status is unverified. All main text was read; two complete paragraphs were inspected in rendered pages. This single-analyst account concerns the accessible version, without assuming typographic identity to the proceedings.

The contribution is a conceptual/design argument for shared feedback, supported by a bounded observational case study of ShrEdit use. ShrEdit is the study setting and partial embodiment of the approach, not a newly introduced artifact whose implementation is this paper's central contribution. The report analyzes video from four groups of three previously acquainted designers, focusing on a final ninety-minute design task. It does not supply a randomized comparison demonstrating superiority over the three reviewed systems.

## Actual organization and opening moves

The sequence is Introduction; Awareness in Collaborative Writing Systems, with Quilt, PREP, and GROVE; Mechanisms for Awareness Information, including problems; Shared Feedback, including system, study, observations, and interpretation; Discussion, including the alternative approach and semi-synchronous systems; Conclusions; acknowledgments and references. The empirical analysis sits inside the conceptual argument rather than occupying a conventional Results section.

The abstract establishes awareness as a coordination concern, identifies costs in explicit information-generation mechanisms, describes a study suggesting an alternative, and offers a qualified promise for collaboration. The introduction starts from collaborative-writing research, defines awareness, distinguishes content from character of activity, and introduces active versus passive provision. It then extends the question beyond synchronous use and previews the argumentative sequence. There is no numbered RQ or contribution list. An assertive claim that the approach benefits other domains is broader than the directly observed writing/design task and should not be copied as an evidence standard.

Related work performs two successive jobs. System descriptions expose concrete mechanism choices; the next section abstracts these into informational and role-restrictive models. Three problems follow: unequal effort/benefit, mismatch between sender-selected information and recipient needs, and restricted access to relevant information. This makes the gap mechanistic rather than chronological. The later discussion reconnects observations to coordination studies and proposes persistent workspaces, so literature also supports transfer after the case evidence.

## Claim, evidence, warrant, and boundary

The main claim is that shared feedback can support flexible coordination without preassigning rigid roles or requiring separate awareness reporting. Video observations show transitions between independent and tightly coupled work, negotiated authorship, and user-created conventions. Transcribed episodes make the coordination mechanism inspectable. The warrant connects visible contributions and peripheral monitoring to opportunistic adjustments in activity. Debriefing comments support participants' interpretations and identify awkward interface controls; they are not interchangeable with observed behavior.

The authors preserve problems: participants still verbally announce activities, cursor visibility is missing, and find/track functions receive little use. Audio/video links remain important. Low use of private windows is qualified by possible experimental production pressure. These details bound the argument that passive shared feedback eliminates effort. The extension to asynchronous persistent traces is conceptual extrapolation, supported by examples such as change bars, rather than another evaluated condition.

## Transferable writing choices

Separate system descriptions, mechanism abstraction, and observed use so readers can test the warrant between them. Preserve workarounds and negative observations inside the argument. Explicitly distinguish demonstrated activity from proposed generalization. This is a useful hybrid conceptual/empirical structure, not evidence that every conceptual paper needs a user study or that every system paper should omit implementation detail.
