# P36 — Data Integration as Coordination

**Source and scope.** Andrew B. Neang, Will Sutherland, Michael W. Beach, and Charlotte P. Lee, *Data Integration as Coordination: The Articulation of Data Work in an Ocean Science Collaboration*, CSCW3 2020, article 256, DOI 10.1145/3432955. Read the 25-page proceedings-formatted PDF from the NSF public-access repository; main text ends p. 22. Official [CSCW 2020 awards](https://cscw.acm.org/2020/awards/) verifies Honorable Mention. Selected for scientific work/infrastructure and a process-oriented alternative to barrier inventories. Single-analyst coding is limited to this version.

**Contribution and support.** The knowledge object is an empirical interpretation of data integration as coordination that can create new research relationships and problems. Interviews with 43 researchers across 22 laboratories, supplemented by more than 20 observations, support six collaborative scenarios. Open/axial coding and iterative memoing are analytic procedures. The authors explicitly state in the introduction that they are applying, not refining, articulation-work and synergizing concepts. The CMAP infrastructure is the setting and an ongoing collaborative design effort, not an evaluated artifact contribution in this paper. Neither the data nor the conceptual application should be relabeled as a new dataset or original grand theory.

**Actual architecture.** Introduction → Literature Review (sharing/reuse/integration; articulation work) → Research Site and Methods (oceanography; collaboration; methods; analysis) → Findings (three forms of distance; integration practices; observationalist–observationalist scenarios; observationalist–modeler scenarios) → Discussion: Data Integration as Articulation Work → Conclusion → Acknowledgements. The abstract starts with the field's sharing focus, identifies the study, previews coordination work before and during technical integration, and ends with implications for how scientific problem spaces form. The introduction (pp. 1–3) moves from data-centric promises to known barriers, then introduces a counterpoint: data integration nevertheless happens. It reframes the question from whether/when sharing occurs to how people accomplish it, defines the conceptual lens and its limits, explains the site, asks one explicit research question, previews findings, and gives a roadmap.

**Related-work argument.** The literature review distinguishes publication/sharing from actual reuse and integration, and shows that contextual information and standards themselves require work. It does not deny that barriers or incentives matter. Articulation-work scholarship supplies distinctions among local work, metawork, and synergizing, followed by the idea of constructing doable scientific problems. These concepts prepare readers to interpret the scenarios. The paper avoids treating the existence of a familiar concept as proof that the local account is redundant: its contribution is what examining a nascent integration effort through that concept reveals.

**Claim–support–warrant–limit.** Cases show negotiation over methods, deliberate boundaries against scooping, joint upstream sampling, modelers' metadata uncertainties, changed production practices, and intermediary roles. The warrant is that interoperable data depend on coordination of tasks, people, and purposes; file availability alone does not accomplish that coordination. The scenario sequence contrasts different relationships rather than presenting six interchangeable examples. Discussion explicitly permits impersonal repository exchange when prior coordination has become embedded and invisible. The findings concern a nascent, foundation-supported ocean-science collaboration, not all scientific data exchange. No standalone limitations section appears, so a writer must preserve this context in claim scope. Suggested situated design approaches are implications, not comparative proof against every standardization initiative.

**Paragraph micro-analysis.** Visually verified p. 20, §5.1, opening “This is not to”: seven sentences. S1 qualifies the preceding critique by allowing impersonal data exchange. S2 supplies a scholarly explanation through historically embedded interoperability work. S3 identifies a misleading retrospective inference that documentation alone enabled success. S4 exposes the prior decisions hidden by that inference. S5 synthesizes these decisions as necessary earlier coordination. S6 compresses the account into a transport metaphor. S7 returns explicitly to the studied researchers and names articulation work as the mechanism. Relations: qualification → conceptual support → alternative explanation → correction → synthesis → analogy → scope anchor. The preceding paragraph critiques isolated deposit-and-retrieve accounts; the next discusses emerging professional roles. This concession-led opening improves precision rather than restating the final claim first.

**Transfer and exception.** Turn a familiar barrier list into a process question when the contribution concerns how people actually overcome, negotiate, or sometimes maintain barriers. Select cases to vary meaningful relationships and explain what each contributes to the comparison. Do not transform these purposively chosen scenarios into representative frequencies, or claim theoretical invention when the authors explicitly disclaim it. A vivid metaphor can consolidate an already-supported mechanism; it should not substitute for that mechanism or force all papers into a fixed prose rhythm.
