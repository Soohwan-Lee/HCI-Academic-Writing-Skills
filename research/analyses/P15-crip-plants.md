## Section architecture

The paper proceeds through introduction, related work, design and exhibits, methodology, findings, discussion, and conclusion. Design and exhibits describe the installation and three settings before the method section explains the autoethnographic inquiry. Methodology addresses first-person research, records and analysis, and positionality. Findings have three thematic branches; discussion develops three corresponding directions for HCI practice.

This is neither a standard system-design/evaluation pair nor a neutral chronology of making. The artifact account establishes the material context; method explains how experience becomes analytical material; findings alternate incidents, interpretations, and theory; discussion articulates what the resulting perspective changes. Related work also continues to do analytical work within findings, rather than disappearing after an introductory literature section.

## Abstract and introduction

The abstract (p. 1) starts with method, object, and conceptual intersection. It identifies the evidential emphasis on breakdowns behind apparently finished exhibits, previews the thematic organization, states the relational interpretation, and closes with implications for design practice and exhibition settings. It does not begin with a generic societal background or promise an intervention effect.

The introduction (p. 1) opens directly with the project and analytic lens. It then situates more-than-human scholarship and the additional disability perspective. A second paragraph describes the installation and its geographically situated exhibits. The final paragraph specifies the two-year source of experience, the first author's role, a research question, the findings' focus, and discussion directions. This supplies an explicit RQ, but after the object and perspective are already established. It is a counterexample to requiring every introduction to withhold its approach until after a long background–gap sequence.

## Related-work argument

Section 2 (pp. 1–3) has two major strands: more-than-human HCI and disability scholarship concerning environments. The first establishes intellectual lineages, then recognizes critiques of universalized human/nonhuman relations and foregrounds critical and non-Western perspectives. The second brings a relevant body of scholarship into HCI, organized through material entanglements, interdependence/care, and purification. Those concepts equip the later analysis.

The gap is thus not an empty topical box. It is a perspective underdeveloped within a particular conversation, despite substantive scholarship elsewhere. This distinction is crucial: the paper can contribute a bridge without claiming to originate disability/environment scholarship. Its own prior artifact publication is acknowledged both in related work and methodology, clarifying the incremental contribution.

## Claim, evidence, and warrant

The authors use photographs, videos, field notes, and approximately 70 pages of journal entries, with iterative reading of theory and data (§4.2, pp. 4–5). The analysis is openly theory-informed rather than presented as wholly inductive discovery. Positionality explains why particular connections are available to the researchers; it is relevant to knowledge production, not merely a demographic appendix.

In §5 (pp. 5–11), each major theme begins with an illness narrative, followed by project incidents and interpretive connections. The deliberate shift from collective authorial voice to the first author's “I” marks whose experience is being reported. Examples include caring for plants and equipment while managing bodily needs, variable sensor interactions, and tensions between living ecologies and polished exhibition conventions. The paper's warrant rests on situated, reflexive interpretation and conceptual connections, not representativeness or causal treatment comparison.

The discussion (§6, pp. 11–13) extends these observations into an argument for disability perspectives, different exhibition practices, and adaptation in design. These are reasoned invitations and strategies, not validated universal interventions. There is no standalone limitations section. An analytical boundary remains necessary: the account concerns one researcher's embodied experiences across a particular project and cannot stand for all disabled people or all more-than-human design. Nor should first-person illness accounts be converted into independently established biomedical mechanisms. Absence of a formal limitations heading is a descriptive observation, not advice to omit scope.

## Paragraph micro-analysis A: positioning a conceptual bridge

**Locator:** §2.2, p. 2, first paragraph; opening “In this paper, we bring”. Three sentences:

| Sentence | Rhetorical role | Connection |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Names the additional perspective offered to the target conversation. | Topic and contribution framing. |
| S2 | Distinguishes limited uptake in HCI from existing work in disability scholarship. | Qualified gap; prevents erasure of precedent. |
| S3 | Explains what importing that scholarship enables and how it informs the analysis. | Warrant and forward bridge. |

The sequence is **perspective → located gap with acknowledgment → analytical use**. It follows a paragraph about critical and non-Western scholarship used in designing the installation. It leads into theoretical literature that makes the proposed bridge substantive. This topic-first paragraph works because its later sentences delimit and justify the opening, rather than merely restating it.

## Paragraph micro-analysis B: an incident before its interpretation

**Locator:** §5.2.1, p. 8, first paragraph; opening “I first noticed”. The paragraph spans the two columns. Six sentences:

| Sentence | Rhetorical role | Connection |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Introduces a failure encountered during a demonstration to a friend. | Situated event, not an abstract thesis. |
| S2 | Establishes the expected sensor/display behavior. | Baseline for the anomaly. |
| S3 | Reports that the friend's touch did not produce it. | Contradiction of expectation. |
| S4 | Gives the author's tentative diagnosis and immediate test. | Response to the anomaly. |
| S5 | Reports successful triggering by the author. | Surprise that complicates the diagnosis. |
| S6 | Repeats the contrast when the friend tries again. | Reinforces the unresolved discrepancy. |

This is **incident → expectation → discrepancy → test → contrasting response → recurrence**. The preceding framing paragraph states the theme's purpose; the selected paragraph lets the reader encounter the puzzle. The next explains the sensing configuration, and subsequent paragraphs develop calibration and situated-data interpretations. Moving the eventual theoretical conclusion into S1 would change this intentionally experiential argument. Paragraph coherence can depend on a question carried forward, not only a conclusion announced first.

## Transfer and counterexample

Preserve who knows, how they came to know, and how an episode supports an interpretation. Distinguish recorded incident, retrospective meaning, theoretical connection, and broader proposition. A coherent first-person passage may be narrative or abductive, while its surrounding section supplies the analytical contract. These choices contrast with Rico's reusable-resource validation: the two papers need different evidence, different section organization, and different sentence-level moves despite both involving designed artifacts.
