# Sections as an argument

Use a section because it performs an intellectual job, not because a venue appears
to require a fixed sequence. Confirm the actual track's instructions separately.
A useful outline records **reader question → answer promised → evidence available
→ next dependency**. Related paragraphs should advance this sequence rather than
restate the importance of the topic at each heading.

## Abstract: compressed contribution, with its warrant

A practical default is problem or phenomenon → unresolved issue → approach and
scope → central result/argument → consequence. Combine moves when space is tight.
Lead directly with the question or contribution when background is already familiar.
For an empirical abstract, identify the studied people/context/comparison enough to
interpret the result. For a system, state the new capability and what evaluation
actually establishes. For a review, identify the corpus and synthesis operation;
for an essay, state the argument and how it is developed. Do not force an essay
into “we conducted a study,” or a design inquiry into an effectiveness claim.

Draft the abstract after locating the paper's decisive evidence. Check each sentence
against the body: does it promise a contribution actually delivered? Does a positive
headline hide a cost, unresolved comparison or restricted population that changes
its meaning? Cut generic significance before cutting these boundaries. Include
numbers only when verified and informative; an abstract need not enumerate every
sample or methodological step.

Contrast [P03, To Trust or to Think](https://doi.org/10.1145/3449287), whose result
includes a performance/experience tension, with [P50, Research Through Design](https://doi.org/10.1145/2207676.2208538),
whose abstract states an essay's argumentative outcomes. The shared task is a clear
contribution contract; the evidence language differs.

## Introduction: reasons that earn the research

An adaptable sequence is:

1. Establish the phenomenon and stakes using specific context.
2. Explain the consequential problem, tension or contested assumption.
3. Credit relevant approaches and what they establish.
4. Identify the remaining uncertainty, limitation or alternative interpretation.
5. State the question or argument and why this approach can address it.
6. Preview the main finding, artifact or insight at its supported scope.
7. Explain the contribution and whom it enables to understand or do something.

This is a planning aid, not seven mandatory paragraphs. Background and problem can
share a paragraph; the gap can emerge through a contrast; questions may appear
later after necessary concepts. Results can precede a contribution list or be woven
into it. A contribution list should distinguish knowledge advances, not list tasks
such as “we interviewed,” “we built,” and “we evaluated.”

[Smit Desai's six-part introduction recipe](https://thesmitdesai5.medium.com/how-to-write-an-hci-introduction-a-six-part-recipe-0a4972dd05b5)
is practitioner guidance, useful as a draft scaffold rather than corpus-derived
law. [P60, Explaining Models](https://doi.org/10.1145/3301275.3302310) places formal
questions in a later study overview. [P48, Implications for Design](https://doi.org/10.1145/1124772.1124855)
opens a conceptual disagreement about how knowledge is valued. Neither requires
inventing an unstudied population to make a gap.

For paragraph planning, give each paragraph a distinct job and write a one-sentence
reason it follows its predecessor. If that reason is only “another aspect of the
topic,” reconsider the order or scope. End the introduction with enough specificity
that a reader could recognize whether the paper delivered its promise.

## Related work: build the warrant for this particular contribution

Start with the question the section must answer. Common jobs include distinguishing
competing explanations, motivating a construct or method, identifying what existing
systems can do, locating a design tradition, or making a conceptual controversy
legible. Group by these jobs instead of authors, chronology or application labels
unless history itself is the argument.

For each cluster, prepare a small working comparison: **shared question → established
knowledge/capability → relevant differences → unresolved issue → consequence for
this paper**. Use this to write synthesis paragraphs, not a sequence of annotated
bibliography entries. Credit the nearest predecessor accurately. A new context
matters when it changes assumptions, experiences or consequences; the mere absence
of an exact combination of keywords is a weak gap.

A productive local pattern is **synthesis claim → contrasting evidence → limitation
or tension → positioning consequence**. Not every paragraph needs a gap-ending
sentence: some define the concepts that make the later gap understandable. Avoid
repeated “however, little work...” endings when the actual distinctions differ.
Say whether a prior method is unsuitable for your purpose, rather than globally
ineffective, when the evidence only supports the narrower claim.

[P53, Recollecting Craft](https://doi.org/10.1145/3610041) organizes scholarship in
two directions between craft and computation. [P64, Typing](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174220)
separates historical assumptions, modern devices and measurement choices. [P51,
Futures Review](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713759) distinguishes background
concepts from the corpus subsequently analyzed. A conceptual essay may instead
integrate literature throughout, as P48 does. Late related work can be sensible
when readers need the artifact's vocabulary first; check the artifact route.

**Original hypothetical rewrite:** “A studied group chat. B studied explanations.
C studied dissent. No one combines them.” becomes: “Existing systems make individual
recommendations interpretable, but group members must also decide whose reasons
enter the discussion. Work on dissent identifies unequal opportunities to challenge
a majority. Together these strands motivate examining how an explanation interface
redistributes opportunities to question a recommendation.” This is a structural
illustration: replace every substantive premise with verified literature before
using it in a manuscript. It does not assert that these literature claims are true.

## Methods, findings and discussion: keep their different jobs

Methods explain how the evidence was produced and why it can answer the question.
Findings/results establish what was learned, including variation and counterevidence.
Discussion explains how this changes understanding, practice or future inquiry.
An integrated design narrative can alternate these jobs, but each transition still
needs to make the inferential role clear.

A useful discussion chain is **finding → interpretation → relation to prior work
→ consequence → condition or unresolved issue**. Do not merely repeat the result
with stronger adjectives. A recommendation needs a warrant: what evidence makes
this action plausible, for whom and under what conditions? A proposed design is
not a tested intervention. An ethnographic account need not end in product features;
P48 explicitly disputes that universal requirement.

Place consequential limitations near the claims they qualify, then consolidate
cross-cutting boundaries when useful. Explain the consequence of a limitation,
not only its label: recruitment limits whose practices are represented; a proxy
limits the construct inferred; a short deployment leaves persistence uncertain.
End with the actual intellectual gain and its scope, rather than a new claim or
an unearned prediction of transformative impact.
